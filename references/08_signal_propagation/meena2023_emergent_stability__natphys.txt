 1   The stable functionality of networked systems is a hallmark of their natural ability
 2   to coordinate between their multiple interacting components. Yet, strikingly, real-
 3   world networks seem random and highly irregular, apparently lacking any design for
 4   stability. What then are the naturally emerging organizing principles of complex-
 5   system stability? Encoded within the system’s stability matrix, the Jacobian, the
 6   answer is obscured by the scale and diversity of the relevant systems, their broad
 7   parameter space, and their nonlinear interaction dynamics. To make advances, we
 8   introduce here the dynamic Jacobian ensemble, which allows us to systematically
 9   investigate the fixed-point dynamics of a range of relevant network-based models.
10   Within this ensemble, we find, complex systems exhibit discrete stability classes,
11   from asymptotically unstable, where stability is unattainable, to sensitive, in which
12   stability abides within a bounded range of the system’s parameters. Most cru-
13   cially, alongside these two classes, we uncover a third class, asymptotically stable,
14   in which a sufficiently large and heterogeneous network acquires a guaranteed stabil-
15   ity, independent of its microscopic parameters, and therefore insensitive to external
16   perturbation. Hence, in this ensemble, two of the most ubiquitous characteristics
17   of real-world networks - scale and heterogeneity - emerge as natural organizing
18   principles to ensure fixed-point stability in the face of changing environmental con-
19   ditions.
20   The study of complex social, biological and technological systems, is often directed towards
21   dramatic events, such as cascading failures 1–5 or abrupt state transitions. 6–10 In reality, however,
22   these represent the exception rather than the rule. In fact, the truly intriguing phenomenon
23   is that, despite enduring constant perturbations and local obstructions, many systems continue
24   to sustain reliably stable dynamics. 11–14 This is achieved in the absence of a detailed design,
25   as indeed, the dynamics of the majority of complex systems are mediated by random, often
26   extremely heterogeneous, networks, comprising a large number of interacting components, and
27   driven by a vast space of microscopic parameters. What then are the roots of this observed
28   stability?
29   To address this we seek the system’s linear stability matrix, namely its Jacobian J, whose


                                                           1
30   principal eigenvalue λ determines its response to perturbation. 15,16 According to linear stability
31   theory, perturbations may either grow exponentially (Re(λ) > 0), capturing instability, or decay
32   exponentially (Re(λ) < 0), if the system is stable. These distinct behaviors depend on the
33   detailed structure of J, and therefore predicting complex system stability boils down to two
34   crucial challenges:
35   Theoretical. The first challenge is of mathematical nature - how do we uncover the structure of
36   J, given the scale, diversity and multiple parameters characterizing real-world complex systems?
37   Indeed, for low-dimensional systems, with just few interacting components, J can be calculated
38   explicitly around each of the system’s potential states. Yet, such explicit construction becomes
39   intractable as the number of components N is increased. 17 A common paradigm to bypass this
40   difficulty is to extract J from random matrix ensembles, allowing a simple construction, with
41   well-established spectral properties. 18–20 This approach, however, overlooks emergent patterns
42   in J, rooted in the system’s dynamic mechanisms, that have profound impact on stability. 21
43   Practical. Regardless of our ability to calculate J, it remains unclear how real systems calibrate
44   their structural/dynamic parameters to ensure that this J is stable. Indeed, random matrix
45   theory indicates that, unless specifically designed for stability, a random J is extremely unlikely
46   to have Re(λ) < 0. 18 We, therefore, seek to uncover naturally emerging organizing principles that
47   secure stability without having to fine-tune the system’s vast number of microscopic parameters.
48   Here, we address both challenges by extracting the structure of the Jacobian matrix directly from
49   the system’s intrinsic nonlinear dynamics. We focus on a family of frequently encountered forms
50   of interaction, from which we derive the dynamic Jacobian ensemble, a non-random matrix
51   contruction, in which topology and dynamics are deeply intertwined. Under this ensemble,
52   stability is determined by a small set of analytically attainable parameters that capture the
53   combined contribution of both topology and dynamics. Most crucially, the ensemble includes a
54   broad class of systems, for which the stability of selected fixed-points becomes inevitable in the
55   limit of N → ∞. Such emergent stability, asymptotically independent of the majority of the
56   system’s microscopic parameters, offers the desired natural design principles to ensure complex
57   system stability. 22–24

58   Fixed-point dynamics
59   Consider a complex system of N interacting components (nodes), whose dynamic activities
60   x(t) = (x1 (t), . . . , xN (t))> are driven by pairwise interactions, potentially nonlinear. The sys-
61   tem’s fixed-points xα capture static states, which, unperturbed, remain independent of time.
62   The dynamics in the vicinity of these fixed-point can be examined through the system’s response
63   to small perturbations δx(t), which, in the linear regime, can be approximated by

                                            dδx
                                                = Jδx + O(δx2 ).                                      (1)
                                             dt
64   Here J, an N × N matrix, represents the system’s Jacobian around xα , which approximates,
65   through a set of linear equations, the original nonlinear system’s dynamics in the perturbative
66   limit, i.e. small activity changes x(t) = xα +δx(t). Hence, J’s spectral properties, and specifically
67   its principal eigenvalue λ, are of crucial importance in characterizing the system’s fixed-point
68   behavior.
69   Two factors shape J - the system’s topology, i.e. who interacts with whom, and its internal
70   dynamics, namely what is the nature of these interactions:

 71   Topology. The first ingredient that impacts the structure of J is the network topology A, a
 72   binary matrix (Aij ∈ (0, 1), Aii = 0), which is typically sparse and often highly heterogeneous. 25
 73   As J is designed to capture the direct response between i and j, its off-diagonal terms vanish
 74   in case there is no direct i, j link, i.e. Aij = 0 ⇐⇒ Jij = 0 for all i 6= j. If, however Aij = 1,
 75   then the relevant term is assigned a weight Wij that captures the strength of the i, j linear
 76   dependence. Together, this leads to


                                              J = (A − I) ⊗ W,                                        (2)

 77   where the Hadamard product ⊗ represents matrix multiplication element by element, and I is
 78   the N × N identity matrix. In (2) the network structure (A) determines the non-vanishing off-
 79   diagonal terms in J, and W determined their weights. The diagonal entries Jii are introduced
 80   through the second term in (2), I ⊗ W , where Wii quantifies xi (t)’s self-linear dependence.
 81   Dynamics - the random matrix paradigm. To complete the construction of (2) we must
 82   assign all weights W . In many of the traditional analyses these unknown weights are extracted
 83   from two pre-selected distributions, P0 (w) and P1 (w), capturing the probability density functions
 84   of the diagonal (P0 ) and off-diagonal (P1 ) weights. This gives rise to the Jacobian ensemble
 85   E(A, P0 , P1 ), in which one first sets the topology A, then assigns diagonal and off-diagonal
 86   weights from P0 (w) and P1 (w), respectively (Fig. 1a-c).
 87   As a classic example for this ensemble, we consider May’s 18 construction, in which A is an Erdős-
 88   Rényi (ER) network, the off-diagonal weights are extracted via Wij ∼ N (0, σ 2 ), a zero-mean
 89   normal distribution, and the diagonal entries are taken uniformly as Wii = 1. This captures the
 90   fact that the interaction strengths are potentially random, hence the distributed nature of Wij ,
 91   while the self-dynamics are driven by the system’s intrinsic relaxation timescales, as expressed
 92   by the fixed value of all Wii , here gauged to unity.
 93   More generally, one can consider more realistic Aij , e.g., small-world 26 or scale-free, 19 which
 94   have, indeed, been shown to impact (2)’s spectral properties. Similarly, P0 (w) and P1 (w) can
 95   be conditioned to account for specific patterns that arise from known dynamic processes. For
 96   example, in predator prey relationships a positive Wij must be matched with a negative Wji , 20
 97   capturing the asymmetry in the benefit/loss of the predator and its prey. More complex dynamic
 98   constraints may further impact the statistical properties of W , limiting the Jacobian to a selected
 99   subset of the random matrix ensemble. 21
100   Yet, even under these more grounded constructions, the E(A, P0 , P1 ) ensemble, continues to have
101   two crucial shortcomings: (i) it provides no explicit guidelines on how to connect P0 (w) and P1 (w)
102   with the system’s specific nonlinear interactions; (ii) by assigning A and W independently, it
103   ignores the potential interplay between the network structure and J’s dynamic weights. This
104   stands in sharp contrast with the frequently observed fact that the system’s response patterns
105   emerge from both structure and dynamics. 27–29
106   How then do we appropriately assign the wights W in (2) to capture this interplay? For low
107   dimensional systems of few components, this can be done on a case by case basis, by explicitly
108   calculating all linear dependencies. However, for complex, often highly heterogeneous networks,
109   in which not only is N  1, but all individual nodes are also potentially diverse, we lack a sys-
110   tematic construction of J, limiting our ability to predict complex system fixed-point dynamics.
111   We, therefore, now seek the rules that govern the structure of J, i.e. the relevant J-ensemble,
112   for a set of frequently encountered forms of network dynamics.

145   from the existing random matrix based constructions. On the one hand, the network structure
146   A continues to determine the non-zero entries, similar to the classic ensemble E(A, P0 , P1 ). Also,
147   the typical magnitude of the diagonal entries is determined by the system’s rates parameters

155   based treatment of E(A, P0 , P1 ), accounts for the effect of the system-specific nonlinear in-
156   teraction dynamics. Consequently, in E(A, G, Ω), identical networks may give rise to highly
157   distinctive Jacobian matrices, depending on whether the interactions are, e.g., social, biolog-
158   ical or ecological, or even on the specific fixed-point within each interaction. Indeed, each of
159   these systems/states is characterized by its unique set of exponents Ω, and, hence, its unique
160   fixed-point dynamics (Fig. 1h).

206   Dynamic stability
207   The dynamic stability of (3) around a given fixed-point is governed by J’s principal eigenvalue λ,
208   requiring that Re(λ) < 0. To obtain λ, let us first focus on the role of the network topology A, G.
209   The ingredients of J ∈ E(A, G, Ω), as expressed in Eqs. (4) and (5), suggest that λ is strongly
210   linked to the network’s weighted degree density function P (d). This is indicated directly through
211   the dependence on di and dj , but also indirectly through the nearest neighbor degree dnn , whose
212   magnitude depends on the system’s degree-heterogeneity. 42 For instance, in a randomly wired
213   network we have dnn = hd2 i/hdi, 7,19 in which the second moment hd2 i increases with P (d)’s
214   variance, and consequently with A, G’s heterogeneity. In case P (d) is fat-tailed, we have 42


                                                  dnn ∼ N β ,                                        (7)

215   an asymptotic divergence with system size. Hence, β is driven by the network’s degree-heterogeneity,
216   being β = 0 for homogeneous networks, in which P (d) is concentrated around its mean, and
217   β > 0 for heterogeneous A, G, where the variance is unbounded.

239   Stability classifier. The stability classifier S in (9) helps group all J ∈ E(A, G, Ω) into distinct
240   stability classes. It achieves this by identifying the relevant topological (β) and dynamic (Ω)
241   control parameters that help analytically predict the stability of any system within the form of
242   Eq. (3). We can therefore use S to predict a priori whether a specific combination of topology
243   and dynamics will exhibit stable functionality or not.
244   The derivation of S in Supplementary Section 3, relies on a star-approximation, in which we
245   reduce the complexity of a degree-heterogeneous network into that of a single hub, i.e. a star
246   network. This, of course, represents a rather crude simplification, and therefore, to examine
247   S’s predictive power in an actual network setting, we tested it extensively against a diverse set
248   of complex networks. Specifically, we used our model and empirical networks to extract 7, 387
249   Jacobian matrices from the E(A, G, Ω) ensemble, with different sets of η, µ, ν and ρ. In Fig. 4a
250   we show the principal eigenvalue λ vs. S for the entire 7, 387 Jacobian sample. As predicted,
251   we find that the parameter S sharply splits the sample into three classes. The asymptotically
252   unstable class (red, top-right) has S > 0 and consequently also λ > 0, a guaranteed instability.
253   The asymptotically stable class (blue, bottom-left) is observed for S < 0, and has, in all cases
254   λ < 0, i.e. stable dynamics. Finally, for S = 0 we observe sensitive stability, with λ having
255   no asymptotic positive/negative assignment (green). A small fraction (∼ 8%) of our sampled J
256   matrices were inaccurately classified by S (grey), an expected consequence of the approximate
257   nature of S’s derivation.

264   The ingredients of dynamic stability
265   The parameter S in (9) reduces Eq. (3)’s dynamic stability into five relevant exponents. The first
266   four Ω = (η, µ, ν, ρ) are determined by the system’s intrinsic dynamics Mq (x), around each of
267   its fixed-points. The remaining exponent in (9), β, is independent of the dynamics, determined
268   solely by A, G, specifically by the weighted degree density function P (d), through (7). Therefore,


                                                       7
269   together, S captures the roles of both topology and dynamics, whose interplay determines the
270   system’s stability class around a specific fixed-point - stable, unstable or sensitive.
271   The only remaining factor in (8) is the coefficient C(f , g), whose value is driven by Eq. (3)’s
272   rate parameters f , g. Yet, as our analysis indicates, this factor is sidelined for large N when
273   S 6= 0. We interpret this to mean that under asymptotic stability or instability, the system’s
274   countless microscopic parameters turn irrelevant, and the stable/unstable fixed-points of (3)
275   become ingrained into the system’s intrinsic dynamics, i.e. the functional form of Mq (x); see
276   Methods Section 2 and Supplementary Section 1 for an expanded discussion on this distinction.
277   To gain deeper insight, consider, for example, the factors that drive a system towards the
278   loss of stability. Most often such events result from external stress or changes in environmental
279   conditions. 7 Such forces impact the system by perturbing its dynamic parameters, e.g., changing
280   the rates of specific processes. Seldom, however, do these environmental perturbations affect
281   the system’s built-in interaction mechanisms. Indeed, these mechanisms are ingrained in the
282   physics of the interacting components, and therefore they are unaffected by external conditions.
283   Hence, asymptotic stability (S < 0, N → ∞) depicts robust dynamic states, that are insensitive
284   to changes in the system’s microscopic parameters.
285   The role of degree-heterogeneity. The prevalence of fat-tailed P (d) is among the defining
286   features of many real-world complex systems, from biological 43 to social 44,45 and technologi-
287   cal 46,47 networks, with far-reaching implications on their observed dynamic behavior. 7,19,27 Our
288   analysis indicates that this universal network characteristic may also play a crucial role in the
289   context of dynamic stability.
290   To understand this, consider a homogeneous P (d), such as an ER network with randomly
291   assigned weights. Here the weighted degrees follow a Poisson distribution, having β = 0. Under
292   these conditions we have S = 0 in (9), the system has no defined asymptotic behavior, and hence
293   it is sensitively stable - i.e. its stability depends on model parameters via C(f , g). Therefore,
294   our predicted asymptotically stable/unstable classes, which depend on β > 0, emerge as a direct
295   consequence of degree-heterogeneity. This suggests that degree/weight-heterogeneity serves as
296   a dynamically stabilizing structural characteristic, locking-in specific fixed-points, in the face of
297   a persistently fluctuating environment.
298   To further uncover the roots of asymptotic stability/instability, we consider again E(A, G, Ω)’s
299   principal eigenvalue λ in (8). Its structure portrays stability as a balance between the posi-
300   tive, i.e. destabilizing, effect mediated by the network interactions, vs. the negative, stabilizing,
301   feedback, driven by the parameter C(f , g) in J’s diagonal, as expressed in Eq. (4); Fig. 4b. It
302   is, therefore, natural to enhance stability by increasing C(f , g), which, in effect, translates to
303   strengthening each node’s intrinsic negative feedback. Equation (8) predicts that J becomes
304   stable if C(f , g) exceeds a critical value

345   Emergent stability. The stabilizing/destabilizing effect of N and P (d) is especially relevant
346   if (3) exhibits multiple fixed-points, for example, an undesirable x0 and a desirable x1 . For a
347   low-dimensional system (N small) transitions between these points are controlled by the system
348   parameters. This is also the case if the system is large (N → ∞) but homogeneous (β = 0).
349   Indeed, both cases belong to the sensitively stable class, in which λ in (8) shows no asymptotic
350   trend - either due to the finite value of N or due to the fact that β = 0 in (9). Such parameter-
351   driven state transitions capture the classic view of dynamic stability: the potential fixed-points
352   are rooted in the system’s intrinsic nonlinear dynamics, their stability, on the other hand, is
353   determined by its specific parameters. 7
354   The picture, however, changes fundamentally when the network is large and heterogeneous, i.e.
355   N → ∞ and β > 0. This is because in E(A, G, Ω) each of the system’s fixed-points may be

356   potentially characterized by a unique set of dynamic exponents, x0 ’s Jacobian having the set
357   Ω0 and x1 having Ω1 (Fig. 5a,b). If, for instance, Ω0 has S > 0 in (9) and Ω1 has S < 0, then

360   To observe this we return to our testing ground of Fig. 2, this time focusing on dynamic models

365   First, we simulated Regulatory on an ER network, and varied the model’s two parameters fi ,
366   the individual node degradation rate, and g, the global interaction strength. We find that when
367   the average hf i is large or, alternatively, when g is small the system resides in x0 , whereas in

374   Therefore, on a large (N → ∞) scale-free (β > 0) network, x0 becomes asymptotically unstable,
375   and the system is forced to reside in the asymptotically stable x1 .
376   To observe this systematically we seek the critical global weight gc , below which x1 becomes
377   unstable, and the system transitions to x0 . Varying the system size N over 4 orders of mag-
378   nitude, from 10 to 2 × 104 , we observe first hand x1 ’s asymptotic stability: while under ER gc

385   The role of the hub nodes. Taken together, we observe a qualitative difference between
386   homogeneous vs. fat-tailed P (d), in which degree-heterogeneity can potentially afford the net-
387   work a guaranteed stability, that is asymptotically independent of microscopic parameters. This
388   phenomenon is rooted in the dominance of the hub nodes, whose dynamic behavior forces the
389   entire system towards stability/instability. In that sense, one can think of our classifier S as a
390   mathematical tool to predict precisely what will be the dynamic role of the hubs - whether the
391   hubs serve as stabilizers (S < 0), destabilizers (S > 0) or neither (S = 0).
392   Generality and limitations the E(A, G, Ω) ensemble. Our ensemble E(A, G, Ω) was an-

417   Discussion and outlook
418   The linear stability matrix J carries crucial information on the dynamic behavior of complex
419   systems. Here, we exposed distinct patterns in the structure of J that (i) arise from the nature
420   of the system’s interaction dynamics; (ii) affect its principal eigenvalue, and thus, its stability
421   profile. These patterns are expressed through the four dynamic exponents Ω = (η, µ, ν, ρ), which
422   we link analytically to the system’s dynamic functions, Mq (x), independently of the weighted
423   network topology A, G or parameters f , g. We interpret this to mean that Ω is hardwired into
424   the system’s innate interaction dynamics, determined by the dynamic model, e.g., Epidemic or
425   Regulatory, but not by the specific model parameters or the system’s underlying connectivity
426   patterns. Therefore, our predicted Jacobian ensemble in (4) and (5), as well as its associated
427   stability classifier S in (9), both capture highly robust and distinctive characteristics of the
428   system’s dynamics, that cannot be perturbed or otherwise affected by shifting environmental
429   conditions.
430   Our predicted asymptotic stability/instability is driven by the limit of large d, i.e. the hubs.
431   It is therefore mainly relevant for degree-heterogeneous networks. While extreme heterogeneity
432   is, indeed, common in many biological and social systems, there are areas, such as in ecological
433   systems, 53 were the networks tend to be more homogeneous. Under such conditions, our theory
434   predicts that the system is in the sensitive class: it could be stable, but its stability is not
435   guaranteed in the face of parameter perturbation.
436   Our asymptotic predictions capture the system’s global stability, but have no bearing on the
437   dynamic stability of small motifs or sub-networks, which may be locally unstable. Still in an
438   asymptotically stable system, the global impact of such unstable motifs, vanishes in the limit
439   of large N , and hence the system as a whole remains insensitive to these local discrepancies
440   (Supplementary Section 6).
441   Graph spectral analysis represents a central mathematical tool to translate network structure
442   into dynamic predictions. 54–56 A network’s spectrum, i.e. its set of eigenvalues and eigenvectors,
443   captures information on its dynamic timescales, potential states, and - in the present context -
444   its dynamic stability. Most often, spectral analysis is applied to the network topology, namely
445   we seek the graph’s eigenvalues, thus overlooking information on the nonlinear dynamics that

                                                      11
446   occur on that graph. As an alternative, our E(A, G, Ω) ensemble suggests to apply spectral
447   analysis, not to the topology A (or the weighted A ⊗ G), but rather to the derived Jacobian in
448   (4) and (5), which, thanks to Ω preserves the information of both structure and dynamics.
449   In a broader perspective, the interplay between topology and dynamics is one of the major
450   theoretical obstacles along the path to predict, understand and control complex system be-
451   havior. 57–60 The problem is that we lack sufficient theoretical tools to treat the challenging
452   combination of nonlinear dynamics with complex, random and often highly heterogeneous net-
453   work structures. Consequently, advances are often system specific, requiring dedicated tools,
454   and hence, lacking the breadth of a general network dynamics theory. The Jacobian ensemble
455   presented here, transforms the nonlinear Eq. (3) into an effective linear system, whose weights
456   incorporate the nonlinear structure of Mq (x) through Ω. It can, therefore, allow us to exploit
457   the powerful tools of spectral graph theory, even in this nonlinear dynamic setting. This may
458   offer a basis for systematic analysis of nonlinear network dynamics.

585   In (32) we distinguish between the role of the powers Γqn and that of the coefficients Cqn .
586   The powers, in most cases, characterize the functional form of Mq (x, fqi ), differentiating, for
587   example, between Mq (x, fqi ) ∼ x2 or Mq (x, fqi ) ∼ x/(1 + x). These different functions are
588   designed to represent, mathematically, distinct microscopic mechanisms, e.g., social interactions
589   vs. biological processes. As these mechanisms are ingrained into the physics of the interacting
590   components, we take them, in our formulation, to be fixed and uniform across all nodes/links. In
591   contrast, the coefficients Cqn are often tunable, depending on the particular rates characterizing
592   each node’s dynamics, and hence they depend on the node specific parameters fqi .
593   To better understand this distinction let us consider a specific example of logistic growth, a
594   common mechanism in population dynamics. Within the dynamic framework of Eq. (3) this
595   mechanism is captured by M0 (xi ) = bi xi (1 − xi /ci ), which written in the form (32), provides
596   M0 (xi ) = bi xi − (bi /ci )x2i . Namely the coefficients are C00 = bi and C01 = −bi /ci , and the
597   corresponding powers are Γ00 = 1 and Γ01 = 2. The crucial point is that while bi and ci , i.e.
598   the species growth rate and the system’s carrying capacity, are node dependent, and potentially
599   affected by environmental conditions, the functional form Mq (xi ) ∼ xi (1 − xi ) is intrinsic to
600   logistic growth, and cannot be easily perturbed. This is precisely captured by the separate role
601   of powers vs. coefficients: the tunable parameters bi , ci are expressed only within Cqn , whereas
602   the logistic growth functional form is embedded within the powers Γqn - here describing linear
603   growth (Γ00 = 1) followed by quadratic attenuation (Γ01 = 2) as driven by the intra-species
604   competition.
605   Hence, from a strictly mathematical perspective, we define parameters as the factors affecting
606   the coefficients Cqn in (32), and functional form via the set of participating powers Γqn . Our
607   interpretation of this mathematical distinction is that the powers are more intrinsic than the
608   coefficients. Indeed, in our logistic growth example, the two powers arise from the system’s
609   ingrained driving mechanisms - of growth (linear) vs. competition (quadratic). In contrast, the
610   coefficients depend on the parameters bi , ci , which may assume any value within the logistic
611   growth framework, and can even change due to external conditions.
612   The crucial point is that our Jacobian scaling exponents Ω depend only on the powers Γqn ,
613   and are unrelated to the coefficients Cqn . Hence, in our example, all systems driven by logistic
614   growth (and a matching interaction dynamics) will have similar Ω, regardless of the specific
615   parameters bi , ci . This portrays Ω and its resulting S, as an innate built-in characteristic of the
616   system’s dynamics, detached from its multitude of microscopic parameters. Consequently, our
617   asymptotic stable/unstable classes are intrinsic to the system’s dynamics, insensitive to external
618   perturbation or to microscopic discrepancies.

Figure 1: The dynamic Jacobian ensemble. To predict dynamic stability we seek the system’s stability
matrix J. (a)-(b) The classic approach is to structure J around the network topology A, with weights extracted
from two distributions: P0 (w) for the diagonal entries Jii and P1 (w) for the interactions strengths Jij , i 6= j. (c)
Together this provides J ∈ E(A, P0 , P1 ), a random matrix based construction, whose stability is determined by
the the structure A and the random weights W . (d) In the dynamic J ensemble we seek emergent patterns in J
that arise from the system specific nonlinear dynamics. This is characterized by three factors: (e) The functional
form of Mq (x) (orange) captures the system’s ingrained dynamics, e.g., social, biological or technological. Our
formalism derives the exponents Ω in Eqs. (4) and (5) directly from these three functions. Hence, around a given
fixed-point, Ω is intrinsic to the the system’s dynamics. (f) The system’s microscopic parameters f , g (turquoise)
provide the specific rate-constants for (3)’s dynamic processes. For example, the infection rate in Epidemic
(top), or the degradation rate in Biochemical (bottom). These parameters are potentially influenced by external
conditions, such as changes in social behavior (Epidemic) or temperature (Biochemical). Their impact on J is
encapsulated within the coefficient C(f , g) in Eq. (4). (g) A, G represent the weighted network (purple), which
is expressed in J via the density function P (d), the nearest neighbor degree dnn in (6) and β in (7). (h) The
resulting J-ensemble, E(A, G, Ω), exhibits non-random scaling patterns driven by the the interplay between A, G
and Ω. Similar to the random E(A, P0 , P1 ), the non-vanishing terms correspond to the network links, however,
in contrast to the random weights of E(A, P0 , P1 ), here the weight of the i, j entry depends on di and dj , as well
as on Ω (orange captions). The result is a dynamic ensemble, in which identical networks (A, G) yield highly
distinctive J matrices depending on Mq (x), e.g., Regulatory (left), Epidemic (center) or Biochemical (right). (i)
The stability of J can be reduced to the classifier S in (9), whose value depends only on the degree heterogeneity
(β, purple) and on the dynamic exponents (orange terms), but not the parameters f , g. Therefore, it provides a
robust classification into stable (blue) or unstable (red) dynamics, that are asymptotically insensitive to changes
in f , g. Under S = 0 the system becomes sensitive (green), and then stability is driven precisely by f , g.

Figure 4: Three classes of dynamic stability in real and model networks. We extracted 7, 387 Jacobian
matrices from the E(A, G, Ω) ensemble, using combinations of model/empirical networks with different dynamic
exponents Ω. For each J we calculated the principal eigenvalue λ and the stability classifier S in (9). (a) λ vs. S
for all 7, 387 J-matrices. We observe the three predicted classes: Asymptotically unstable (red) in which S > 0
and hence, as predicted, we have also λ > 0; Sensitively stable (green), where S = 0 and λ can be both positive
or negative; Asymptotically stable (blue), where S < 0 and therefore λ < 0. Our classification was inaccurate in
∼ 4% of the cases on binary networks, ∼ 5% on weighted networks and ∼ 15% on weighted/negative networks,
amounting to a total discrepancy of ∼ 8% (grey dots) over the entire ensemble. (b) The value of λ emerges
from the competition between J’s off-diagonal terms, representing positive feedback, and the strength of the
diagonal terms Jii (negative feedback). Therefore one can force a system towards stability (λ < 0) by increasing
the coefficient C in (4). (c)-(e) Taking three specific J matrices from each class, we plot λ vs. C, seeking the
critical C0 , in which λ becomes negative (grey lines). This represents the critical C, above which stability is
ensured. (f) For the stable JAS (S = −1.2) we find that C0 decreases with N (squares), capturing the asymptotic
stability, in which for large systems (N → ∞) stability is sustained even under arbitrarily small C. The theoretical
scaling predicted in Eq. (10) is also shown (solid line, slope −1.2). (g) For JSS we have S = 0, the critical C0 is
independent of N , hence the system’s stability can be affected by finite changes to its dynamic parameters, e.g.,
environmental perturbations. (h) The asymptotically unstable JAU (S = 1.8) has C0 → ∞ in the limit of large
N , in perfect agreement with Eq. (10) (solid line). Here, for a sufficiently large system (N → ∞), no matter
how large is C, the fixed-point associated with JAU is always unstable. (i)-(k) For a homogeneous P (d), e.g.,
Erdős-Rényi, β vanishes and hence S = 0 in (9). Under these conditions, regardless of Ω, the system is always
sensitively stable and therefore C0 does not scale with N . This demonstrates the role of degree-heterogeneity in
ensuring stability, in the face of changing environmental conditions.

Figure 5: Emergent stability in large heterogeneous networks. (a)-(b) Regulatory dynamics exhibit two
fixed points (3D plots), each with its own J ∈ E(A, G, Ω), shown to the right of each plot. The inactive x0 has
Ω = (0, 0, 0, 0) and S = β > 0, and hence it is asymptotically unstable. The active x1 has a different J, with
Ω = (0, 0, 0, −2) and S = −β < 0, asymptotically stable. (c) The state of Regulatory as obtained from numerical
simulations on our Erdős-Rényi (ER) network under varying g. The system transitions from x1 (right) to x0 (left)
under small g. This represents sensitive stability, as indeed predicted for ER, in which parameters, here g, affect
the state of the system. (d) To examine this systematically we plot the mean activity hxi vs. g as obtained for
ER (diamonds) and for our scale-free network SF1 (triangles). Both systems exhibit a critical gc , below which x1
becomes unstable and the system transitions to x0 . The crucial point is, however, that thanks to its heterogeneity
SF1 exhibits an increased robustness against g variations, with gc an order of magnitude lower than that observed
for ER. (e) hxi vs. hf i shows a similar behavior. Here increasing hf i causes the system to collapse to x0 , and,
as predicted, SF1 can incur higher hf i than ER. (f) The state of SF1 under the same four conditions shown in
panel (c). As predicted, SF1 remains at x1 even when ER has already collapsed to x0 . (g) gc vs. the system size
N as obtained from numerically simulating Regulatory dynamics. For ER we observe gc ∼ const (diamonds),
hence there is a typical gc below which the system transitions to x0 . Consequently x1 can be destabilized via
parameter perturbation. Note that while N spans over four orders of magnitude, gc varies by a mere ∼ 40%. In
contrast, for SF1 we find that gc exhibits negative scaling with N , approaching gc → 0 in the limit N → ∞. This
captures precisely the predicted asymptotic stability, in which a sufficiently large and heterogeneous network is
guaranteed to stably reside in x1 even under arbitrarily small g (or large hf i). (h)-(i) Repeating this experiment
for Epidemic and Inhibitory, we continue to observe our predicted asymptotic stability: under the heterogeneous
SF1 we have gc → 0 as N → ∞, whereas under the homogeneous ER gc is (almost) independent of N .

Building a flash-crash model, starting from one oscillator

This is the whole model in the order you would actually discover it. Each step starts with the simplest thing that could work, shows precisely where it breaks, and adds exactly one term to fix it. By the end you have the equation the repository integrates, you know why every term is there, and you know which parts have been tested against real markets and which have failed.

Step 1

One oscillator, and what a phase even is

Start with a single trading desk. We will not track its cash, its inventory or its positions. We track one number: where it is in a repeating buy-then-sell cycle. Call that angle θ.

(1)dθ/dt = ω ω is the desk's natural frequency: how fast it goes round on its own. Economically it is the inverse of its trading horizon. A high-frequency market maker turning over inventory in seconds has large ω; a pension fund rebalancing quarterly has small ω.

The angle is only useful because it maps onto something observable. Define the desk's net order flow as the projection of the phase:

(2)q = sin θ θ = +90° is peak buying (q = +1), θ = −90° is peak selling (q = −1), and θ = 0° or 180° is flat. This is the only bridge from the abstract angle to a market quantity, and everything later depends on it.
Where this breaks

Nothing happens. One desk cycles forever at a constant rate. There is no interaction, so there is nothing to explain. We need at least two.

Step 2

Two desks that watch each other, and the first threshold

Now let desk 1 see desk 2's order flow and be pulled toward it, and vice versa. The natural pull is proportional to the sine of the phase difference: strong when they are out of step, zero when aligned.

(3) dθ1/dt = ω1 + K sin(θ2 − θ1)
dθ2/dt = ω2 + K sin(θ1 − θ2) K is the coupling strength: how hard each desk copies the other.

Subtract the two equations. Everything collapses onto the phase difference φ = θ2 − θ1:

(4)dφ/dt = Δω − 2K sin φ,    Δω = ω2 − ω1

This tiny equation already contains the whole story. A fixed point exists only if sin φ = Δω/2K has a solution, which needs |Δω| ≤ 2K. So:

(5)they lock  ⇔  |Δω| ≤ 2K Below the threshold they drift apart forever. Above it they snap to a fixed phase offset and move as one. Notice it is a competition: disagreement about pace (Δω) against the urge to copy (K).
The idea to carry forward

Synchronisation is always this fight. Diversity of trading horizons resists it; imitation drives it. Every threshold in the rest of this document is a version of inequality (5).

Step 3

A crowd of desks: the Kuramoto model

Take N desks, each watching every other, with frequencies drawn from a distribution g(ω). Kuramoto's 1975 model divides the coupling by N so the total pull stays finite as the crowd grows:

(6)dθi/dt = ωi + (K/N) Σj sin(θj − θi)

N coupled equations is hopeless directly. The trick that makes it solvable is to summarise the whole crowd in one complex number, the order parameter:

(7)R eiΨ = (1/N) Σj eiθj Think of every desk as a unit arrow on a circle. R is the length of their average arrow and Ψ is its direction. R = 0 means the arrows point everywhere and cancel; R = 1 means every desk is identical. R is the measure of herding.

Substituting (7) into (6) makes each desk decouple from all the others and talk only to the mean field:

(8)dθi/dt = ωi + K R sin(Ψ − θi) This is the same shape as equation (3), with the crowd playing the role of the single partner and KR as the effective pull. So desk i locks when |ωi − Ω| ≤ KR, exactly inequality (5) again.

But R is itself built from the desks, so it must be consistent with what the desks do. Working out that self-consistency gives the classic result: below a critical coupling the only solution is R = 0, and above it R grows continuously from zero.

(9)Kc = 2 / (π g(0)) For a standard-normal g, g(0) = 1/√(2π) = 0.3989, giving Kc = 1.596. Wider frequency spread means smaller g(0), which means a larger Kc: more diversity is harder to synchronise, as it should be.
A scaling law worth remembering

Equation (6) is unchanged if you multiply both ω and K by the same number and slow time down to match. So only the ratio K/Δω is physical. This is why comparing two frequency distributions is meaningless unless you standardise them first, and why the safe-halt result in step 10 comes out proportional to 1/Δω.

Step 4

Why that is not a market yet

Equation (6) is elegant and wrong for our purpose, in four specific ways. The rest of the model is the repair of each one.

  1. Everybody sees everybody. The (K/N)Σj runs over all desks. Real traders watch a handful of others. Fixed in step 5 by putting it on a network.
  2. The transition is smooth. R rises continuously from zero at Kc. Markets do not drift gently into crashes, they snap. And a smooth transition has no memory: reverse the coupling and you retrace the same curve, so a crash would heal the moment its cause was removed. Fixed in steps 6 and 7.
  3. There is no price. Phases are not money. Fixed in step 8.
  4. Nothing ever surprises it. No news, no shocks, no randomness. Fixed in steps 8 and 9.

Step 5

Put the desks on a network

Replace "everyone" with an observation network. Aij = 1 if desk i watches desk j's order flow, 0 otherwise. The number of desks i watches is its degree ki.

(10)dθi/dt = ωi + (λ/⟨k⟩) Σj Aij sin(θj − θi) Dividing by the mean degree ⟨k⟩ rather than N keeps the λ axis comparable across networks with different densities. It is a pure rescaling, so it changes no physics.

Now the structure of the network matters. The quantities that describe it:

quantitydefinitionwhat it measures
⟨k⟩mean degreehow many desks a typical desk watches
κ⟨k²⟩/⟨k⟩² = 1 + cvk² degree heterogeneity. 1 if every desk has identical degree, large when a few hubs dominate
Ci2Ti / (ki(ki−1)) clustering: do the desks I watch also watch each other? Ti counts triangles at i
rdegree correlation across edges assortativity: do hubs connect to hubs, or to the periphery?
λmax(A)largest adjacency eigenvaluesets many spreading thresholds

Redoing the mean-field calculation on a network (each desk now feels a field proportional to its own degree) gives a threshold that depends on structure through κ alone:

(11)λc = 2 / (π g(0) κ) Ichinomiya 2004; Restrepo, Ott & Hunt 2005. Heterogeneity lowers the threshold: hubs feel a much stronger field than average and lock first, seeding the rest.
A falsifiable claim, not a description

Clustering, assortativity and path length do not appear in (11). If they mattered, this formula would fail on a highly clustered network. That is a prediction you can test, and the sweep in the repository does test it.

Where this still breaks

The transition is still continuous. We moved the threshold; we did not change its character. Heterogeneity alone does not produce a crash.

Step 6

What actually makes a transition abrupt

A crash is a first-order transition: R jumps discontinuously, and the forward and backward paths differ, which is hysteresis. Three known mechanisms produce this. They are genuinely different and it matters which you pick.

Route A: correlate frequency with degree

Set ωi ∝ ki. Because the field a desk feels also scales with ki, the desks that are hardest to entrain are exactly the ones with the most pull. Nobody can lock alone, so nothing locks until everything locks at once (Gómez-Gardeñes et al. 2011).

Measured here, and sharper than the textbook version

The sweep in this repository varied the rank correlation c between ω and k while controlling the shape of g(ω) separately. Result: a Gaussian frequency distribution never produces a jump at any correlation (ΔR ≈ 0.08 flat from c = −1 to +1). The jump appears only at |c| = 1 and only when g(ω) has a tail as heavy as the degree distribution (ΔR = 0.578 with a degree-matched marginal, 0.498 with a Lorentzian). Even c = 0.75 gives almost nothing.

So the right statement is not "frequency-degree correlation causes explosive synchronisation". It is: the tail of g(ω) must match the tail of P(k), and a rank correlation is not enough.

Route B: change the shape of g(ω)

Even with all-to-all coupling and no network at all, a uniform frequency distribution makes the Kuramoto transition first-order, with R jumping to exactly π/4 ≈ 0.785 (Pazó 2005). A bimodal distribution does something similar near λ = 2ω0 (Martens et al. 2009). Note g(0) = 0 for a bimodal law, so formula (11) does not even apply.

Route C: make the coupling adaptive

This is the one that fits a market, and it is step 7.

Step 7

The herding term, which is where the crash comes from

Route A needs desks' trading horizons to be proportional to their connectivity, which is an odd thing to assert about a market. Route C assumes something far more natural: you copy harder when the people you are watching already agree with each other.

Measure that agreement locally. For desk i, take the average arrow of just its neighbours:

(12)ri = |Σj Aij eiθj| / ki ri = 0 when i's neighbours are split evenly, ri = 1 when they are unanimous. It is a purely local quantity: desk i can actually observe it.

Now multiply the coupling by it (Zhang, Boccaletti, Guan & Liu 2015):

(13)dθi/dt = ωi + (λ/⟨k⟩) ri Σj Aij sin(θj − θi)
Why this one term changes everything

It is positive feedback. More agreement raises ri, which raises the effective coupling, which produces more agreement. In the plain model (10) the pull is linear in the crowd's coherence; here it is roughly quadratic, and that is enough to turn a smooth transition into a jump with a bistable region.

The mean-field version makes the mechanism explicit. Writing u = λR² for the effective locking strength:

(14)R = H(u) = u ∫−π/2π/2 cos²φ · g(u sin φ) dφ,     λ(u) = u / H(u)² λ(u) diverges as u → 0 and grows again for large u, so it has a minimum. That minimum is a saddle-node bifurcation, and it is the recovery threshold λb.

The consequence is the single most important structure in the model:

regionwhat existsmarket meaning
λ < λbonly R ≈ 0 is stable calm. A shock causes a flash crash that heals by itself
λb < λ < λf two stable states bistable. The same shock can leave the market permanently crashed
λ > λfonly the crashed state crashes with no trigger at all

Measured on the configuration the simulation ships with (ER, ⟨k⟩ = 12, f = 1, σ = 0.3): λb = 3.1, λf = 4.1, hysteresis gap 0.80. You can reproduce this live with the auto-ramp button.

And for Lorentzian frequencies it is exactly solvable

The classic Kuramoto result R = √(1 − Kc/K) with Kc = 2γ enters the mean field as K·R. Here the gain is αi = ri, so it enters as λR², i.e. Keff = λR. Substituting gives a cubic,

λR³ − λR + 2γ = 0

whose fold (double root, 3λR² = λ) sits at R* = 1/√3, giving a closed form for the recovery threshold: λb = 3√3 γ ≈ 5.196γ, with R* = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577 at the fold. Verified numerically: at λ = 5.2, just above the fold, the two roots are 0.5901 and 0.5645, converging on 0.5774 as they must. This replaces the numerical saddle-node search that step 7's mean field used, which was about 10% off.

Hysteresis is the point

A crash persists not because the shock persists, but because the market has moved onto a different stable branch. Whether a shock heals or sticks is a property of where λ sits, not of how big the shock was.

Step 8

From phases to prices, plus noise

Aggregate the order flow, and let the imbalance move the price:

(15)Q = (1/N) Σi sin θi = R sin Ψ
(16)dx = (βQ − κx) dt + η dB,    P = 100 ex β is price impact, κ pulls the price back toward a fundamental value, η is price noise.

Equation (15) is worth staring at. A crash needs R high and Ψ near −90°. R high with Ψ near +90° is a melt-up. The model produces both, symmetrically, which is already a hint that something is missing, since real markets fall faster than they rise.

Finally, two sources of randomness:

(17)… + σ dWi + σc dWc σ: idiosyncratic news, independent for each desk. σc: a common market mode, the same increment for everyone. The second one is not in the original model; step 12 explains why it had to be added.

Step 9

Shocks, and why some crashes stick

A shock is a common sell signal pushing a chosen subset of desks toward θ = −π/2:

(18)… + ε(t) si sin(−π/2 − θi) ε is the shock strength and si marks which desks are hit. The fraction hit is φ.

The same shock, applied at three different values of λ, gives three different markets:

λoutcomedrawdown
below λbV-shaped flash crash, full recovery−7.6%
inside the windowcrash locks in, price stays down−16.2%
ramped past λfcrashes with no shock at all−19.2%

Who you hit matters as much as how hard. Because the field scales with degree, hubs are the efficient target: hitting 10 to 15% of desks chosen as hubs reliably flips the market, while the same number of peripheral desks does almost nothing.

Step 10

Circuit breakers as desynchronisation

If a crash is a synchronised state, a trading halt is an attempt to desynchronise. Cut the coupling and every desk free-runs at its own ω, so the cluster spreads out at a rate set by the diversity of frequencies:

(19)Rhalt(t) = |∫ g(ω) ei[arcsin(ω/u) + ωt] dω| · e−σ²t/2

Trading may resume safely once the herd has spread out past the basin boundary, which gives a minimum safe halt length:

(20)τ*(λ) = min{ τ : Rhalt(τ) < Ru(λ) }   →   τ* = f(λ/λb) / Δω The halt must be longer the more herded the market is, and shorter the more diverse its trading horizons are. Too short and it re-crashes on resumption.
Honest accuracy

This theory reproduces the trend but sits 16 to 44% below the simulated boundary, and halts largely stop working near λf, where noise re-triggers the crash regardless. This is the most novel part of the repository and also the least precise.

Step 11

Now test it on real markets

23 markets, about 2,900 constituent stocks, daily data 2000 to 2026, 324 crash onsets. Two predictions were tested, and both failed.

E1: no early warning

Against random dates several indicators look predictive. Against the fair comparison, ordinary market peaks not followed by a crash, every indicator's confidence interval touches 0.5. Best AUC 0.571. Co-movement measures are flat until the peak and rise only after the fall begins: they react to crashes, they do not anticipate them.

E2: no co-movement hysteresis

The model says co-movement should be higher coming out of a crash than going in, at the same volatility. Measured median gap: 0.0006 to 0.0031, with confidence intervals containing zero. A planted +0.03 effect was recovered, so the test had power to detect 0.03.

E6: the 2010 flash crash, finally, at 1-minute resolution

A free 1-minute feed covering 2002 onward (HF Data Library, CC BY 4.0) reaches 2010-05-06. Across 32 large caps, co-movement rose +0.137 in the 92 minutes BEFORE 14:32 and then fell slightly during the crash itself. On daily data E1 found the reverse: flat until the peak, rising only afterwards.

The null cuts this down, though. Running the same 13:00–14:32 window on every day that week: the crash day has the largest afternoon rise (+0.141), but 2010-05-05 reaches +0.115 and the crash day's absolute level is not even the week's highest (2010-05-07, the aftermath, is). With one crash and four control days, and the whole week inside the European debt crisis, this cannot separate a precursor from ordinary intraday variation. Suggestive, not established.

E7: the timescale objection, settled at the model's own timescale

This was the strongest objection to everything above: the model is intraday, the 2010 flash crash lasted 36 minutes, and these tests used daily closes. So the whole study was redone on 2,319,682 one-minute bars spanning 2002 to 2026, 31 intraday crash events, with E1's own matched null.

The raw result looks spectacular: pre-event co-movement 0.458 before crashes against 0.228 before ordinary peaks, AUC 0.911 [0.843, 0.969], against E1's 0.542 on daily data. Taken at face value, E1's null was a resolution artifact.

It is not. Crashes happen in high-volatility regimes and co-movement rises with volatility, so the unmatched comparison scores well purely as a volatility proxy. Pre-event volatility is 4.94× higher before crashes. Pairing each crash with the nearest-volatility ordinary peak, exactly as E1 did on daily data:

AUC 0.897  →  0.526, CI [0.340, 0.712]

Once volatility is held fixed, co-movement carries no skill at minute resolution either. The same confound structure appears at both timescales. E1's daily null was not a resolution artifact, and E1's conclusion survives the test it was most vulnerable to.

Caveats: 21 crashes survive the pre-window requirement, the matched interval is correspondingly wide, and the cross-section is 23 large caps rather than a full index. A larger ticker set would tighten the interval, but 0.526 is not close to meaningful skill.

But E2 flagged a caveat about itself, and that caveat turns out to be the whole story.

Step 12

The observable gap: the model could not possibly have passed

In the price mapping, each asset's return was driven by its own desk's order flow plus independent noise. In a locked crash every qi is pinned near a constant, so each return is a constant plus independent noise, so the mean pairwise correlation is zero. Not small: zero, as an identity, at every value of R.

What that means

E2 tested a prediction the model never made. Meanwhile real markets are unambiguous: correlation rises from calm to crash in 23 markets out of 23, median +0.127 (0.229 → 0.389). The model produced 0.000. It was failing the most basic co-movement fact before hysteresis was even at issue.

The repair is one parameter: the common market mode σc from equation (17). With it, each asset loads on the shared shock with weight cos θi, and those weights are aligned precisely when the phases are aligned. Writing a = βσc and v for the idiosyncratic return variance:

(21) ui = a cosθi / √(a²cos²θi + v),    ρ̄ = ⟨uiuj⟩ = ⟨u⟩²
weak-mode limit:   ρ̄ ≈ (a²/v) R² cos²Ψ Since ⟨cosθ⟩ = R cosΨ. So ρ̄ ∝ R²: synchrony finally becomes visible in the observable that E2 actually measures.

Simulating this confirms the mechanism and the formula (measured crash co-movement rises 0.0001 to 0.0955 as σc goes 0 to 0.8, tracked by ⟨u⟩² within about a factor of two). Then comes the decisive number:

E2 had no power

Recomputing E2's own gap statistic on the repaired model gives 0.0002 to 0.0028 at every σc. E2's detection bound was 0.013. Even with the mapping fixed, the model predicts an effect roughly four times smaller than the smallest one E2 could have seen.

So E2's null result does not falsify the model's hysteresis. It is a negative result about the test, not about the model.

But one channel is still not enough

σc cannot produce a calm-period baseline at all (0.0006 against a real 0.229). The obvious alternative, a common shock in the prices, has the opposite failure: it matches the level but produces no crash rise (+0.008 against a real +0.127). A calibrated model needs both channels. Neither alone fits.

Step 13

Does the closest prior work hold up?

Lee et al. (PNAS 2025) report the positive version of everything above: that a market's pre-crisis proximity to explosive synchronization predicts whether its collapse and recovery are rapid or prolonged. They validate on 39 country indices around the 2008 crisis. If that is right, the negative results in step 11 need explaining.

Their estimator, implemented here exactly as published: Hilbert-transform each asset's returns to get instantaneous phases, build the Kuramoto order parameter across the market, take the autocorrelation of R(t) inside overlapping moving windows, and use the kurtosis of those ACF values as ES proximity.

The 2008 result does not reproduce at constituent level. This is not an exact replication: they used 39 country indices from Compustat Global, this uses constituent-level data inside 23 markets, so the networks differ and survivorship bias applies here.

Step 14

Can you actually measure the coupling? No, and the reason is the model itself

Everything operational in steps 9 and 10 is phrased as where λ sits relative to λb. None of it can be used unless λ can be measured in a real market. Equation (21) seems to offer exactly that: measure co-movement, invert to get R, then invert the model's own equilibrium curve to get λ.

(23)ρ̄ → R̂ = Rmax √(ρ̄/ρ̄max) → λ̂ = Req−1(R̂)

Applied to 27,723 market-days across 23 markets, it fails, for two independent reasons that are worth more than the method would have been.

1. You cannot invert a step function

Req(λ) jumps from R = 0.137 to R = 0.899 across a λ window only 0.167 wide. That is not a numerical problem, it is the definition of a first-order transition. Every measured R in between is consistent with that whole window, and 97.2% of real market-days land inside it. The estimate ends up with an inter-quartile range of 0.04.

The property that makes explosive synchronization interesting is exactly the property that makes its control parameter unidentifiable from its order parameter.

2. Even a perfect inversion could not help

λ̂ is a monotone transform of ρ̄, and AUC is invariant under monotone transforms. So λ̂ must score identically to raw mean correlation on any ranking test. No reparametrisation of an indicator can ever beat that indicator.

This kills what had been the headline recommendation of step 17. It is kept here because a negative identifiability result is a real constraint on the whole programme: any first-order synchronization model of markets inherits it.

Step 15

Run it on a real market network, and most of the story collapses

Every result so far, including the bistable window that steps 9 and 10 depend on entirely, was measured on a synthetic Erdos-Renyi graph. Nothing in this project had ever used a network estimated from actual market data. That assumption is load-bearing, so it has to be tested.

Two networks were built per market from daily returns and thresholded to the same mean degree (12) as the synthetic benchmark, so degree is held fixed and only structure differs: corr thresholds the return-correlation matrix; leadlag keeps i–j when i's returns lead j's more strongly than the reverse.

The bistable window essentially vanishes

The hysteresis gap falls from 0.793 to 0.17–0.19, a factor of four, and the window width goes to zero. Step 9's crash-persistence story and step 10's circuit-breaker theory both depend entirely on that window existing. On a network estimated from real market data there is no window for a shock to leave the market in, so the most novel results in this project are built on a structure the data does not show.

The likely mechanism is the clustering gap. When neighbours share neighbours, local agreement ri saturates locally instead of propagating globally, which is exactly the positive feedback step 7 depends on. It also means the annealed mean field of step 5, which assumes no clustering, does not apply to the real networks either.

Novelty: the mechanism is known, the application is not

Clustering suppressing explosive synchronization is already published (Topologically-induced suppression of explosive synchronization, Chaos 33, 053103, 2023). So this is not a new physical effect. What is new is the empirical half: networks estimated from real market returns sit squarely in that suppression regime (C = 0.73 against 0.030 for the synthetic graph used throughout), which removes the bistable window steps 9 and 10 depend on. The claim is "the market's own network is the wrong network for this model", not "clustering suppresses explosive synchronization".

Caveats, stated plainly

The real networks are smaller (n = 125 to 335 against 400) and the thresholding is a modelling choice, not an observable. Neither explains a fourfold collapse, but both should be checked: rebuild the synthetic comparison at matched n, and sweep the threshold, before treating this as settled.

Step 16

Could you actually have called a crash? The confusion matrix

Step 11 reported AUC. AUC ranks, and ranking hides the two things that decide whether a signal is usable: the base rate and the confound. So: fix thresholds on 2002–2015, apply them unchanged to 2016–2026, and count actual calls. Ten crashes across 2,697 test trading days, a base rate of 0.37%.

Three detectors. The third is co-movement after regressing out log-volatility, which is the part of synchronization that is genuinely not volatility. That regression alone has R² = 0.414, i.e. 41% of co-movement is just volatility.

No. And the reason is worse than "weak"

1. Nothing is usable. The best detector catches half the crashes at 77 false alarms per catch, precision 1.3% against a 0.37% base rate. At every threshold tested this is operationally worthless.

2. Volatility alone beats the model's own observable. It has the best precision and the fewest false alarms of the three.

3. Removing the confound makes it worse, not better. The part of co-movement that is genuinely not volatility is the worst detector of the three: precision 0.0036, recall 0.20, 274 false alarms per catch. The synchronization-specific component carries no predictive information at all.

Step 11 showed the ranking signal was volatility. This shows that what remains after removing volatility is not merely weak but actively unhelpful, out of sample, at the model's own timescale. That closes the empirical question.

Step 17

Two things this work actually found

Everything up to here either reproduces known work or reports a negative result. These two are positive, quantitative, and were derived and tested here.

Synthetic ER, used for every crash result here: C = 0.030, an order of magnitude below C*. Real market correlation network: C = 0.731, 2.1× above it.

The model's bistable window is an artifact of using an unclustered graph, and the crossing point is now quantified rather than asserted.

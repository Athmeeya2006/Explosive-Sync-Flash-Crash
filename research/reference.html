Building a flash-crash model, starting from one oscillator

This began as one question: if a flash crash is everybody selling at once, can it be modelled as a synchronisation transition, the way a crowd of metronomes on a shared board falls into step? It ends somewhere different from where it set out, and this document is written in the order the work actually happened rather than in the order that would make it look tidy.

Steps 1 to 14 build the model. Each one starts with the simplest thing that could work, shows exactly where it breaks, and adds one term to fix it, so that by the end every term in the equation the repository integrates has a reason to be there. Steps 15 to 21 take that finished model to real market data, and most of what they report is failure: two predictions tested and rejected, one observable that the model could not possibly have produced, one measurement that turns out to be impossible in principle, and one discovery that the network the model runs on is nothing like the network a market actually has. Steps 22 to 24 list every assumption the whole thing rests on, what should be deleted, and which of the proposed repairs were tried and whether they helped. Step 25 traces the arc from the first equation to the last result, including the detours.

Results are labelled throughout by what kind of result they are: reproductions of published work, negative results, and the two things here that appear to be new. Numbers quoted in the text come from the files in research/data/ and research/empirical/data/, and the scripts that produced them are named where they are used.

Three symbols are overloaded

Each of these is standard in its own literature, which is how the collisions arose:

  • κ means degree heterogeneity ⟨k²⟩/⟨k⟩² in equation (11) and the price anchoring rate in equation (16). Unrelated quantities, same letter, both standard in their own literature.
  • φ is the phase difference in equation (4) and the fraction of desks hit by a shock in step 12.
  • r is local agreement ri in equation (12) and degree assortativity in the network table of step 5. λ is also overloaded: the coupling, and λmax(A) the largest adjacency eigenvalue.

What one unit of time means

Nothing in the model is in seconds. The equation has a scaling invariance, shown properly in step 3: multiply ω and λ by the same factor a and run time a times faster, and you get the identical trajectory. So only the ratio λ/Δω is physical, and time is measured in units of 1/Δω.

The repository standardises Δω = 1, which is why λ can be quoted as a bare number at all: λ = 4 means "the coupling is four times the spread of trading horizons", not four of anything. Mapping a time unit onto minutes has never been calibrated (see assumption 32 and, more seriously, assumption 41), so every duration in this document, including the safe halt length τ* in step 14, is in model time.

Step 1

One oscillator, and what a phase even is

Start with a single trading desk. We will not track its cash, its inventory or its positions. We track one number: where it is in a repeating buy-then-sell cycle. Call that angle θ.

(1)dθ/dt = ω ω is the desk's natural frequency: how fast it goes round on its own. Economically it is the inverse of its trading horizon. A high-frequency market maker turning over inventory in seconds has large ω; a pension fund rebalancing quarterly has small ω.

The angle is only useful because it maps onto something observable. Define the desk's net order flow as the projection of the phase:

(2)q = sin θ θ = +90° is peak buying (q = +1), θ = −90° is peak selling (q = −1), and θ = 0° or 180° is flat. This is the only bridge from the abstract angle to a market quantity, and everything later depends on it. Why sin and not cos, and what that choice does and does not fix: step 13.
Where this breaks

Nothing happens. One desk cycles forever at a constant rate. There is no interaction, so there is nothing to explain. We need at least two.

Step 2

Two desks that watch each other, and the first threshold

Now let desk 1 see desk 2's order flow and be pulled toward it, and vice versa. The natural pull is proportional to the sine of the phase difference: strong when they are out of step, zero when aligned.

(3) dθ1/dt = ω1 + K sin(θ2 − θ1)
dθ2/dt = ω2 + K sin(θ1 − θ2) K is the coupling strength: how hard each desk copies the other. The sine is not a convenience. Step 13 shows that a cosine coupling cannot synchronise anything at all, at any K.

Subtract the two equations. Everything collapses onto the phase difference φ = θ2 − θ1:

(4)dφ/dt = Δω − 2K sin φ,    Δω = ω2 − ω1

This tiny equation already contains the whole story. A fixed point exists only if sin φ = Δω/2K has a solution, which needs |Δω| ≤ 2K. So:

(5)they lock  ⇔  |Δω| ≤ 2K Below the threshold they drift apart forever. Above it they snap to a fixed phase offset and move as one. Notice it is a competition: disagreement about pace (Δω) against the urge to copy (K).
The idea to carry forward

Synchronisation is always this fight. Diversity of trading horizons resists it; imitation drives it. Every threshold in the rest of this document is a version of inequality (5).

Step 3

A crowd of desks: the Kuramoto model

Take N desks, each watching every other, with frequencies drawn from a distribution g(ω). Kuramoto's 1975 model divides the coupling by N so the total pull stays finite as the crowd grows:

(6)dθi/dt = ωi + (K/N) Σj sin(θj − θi)

N coupled equations is hopeless directly. The trick that makes it solvable is to summarise the whole crowd in one complex number, the order parameter:

(7)R eiΨ = (1/N) Σj eiθj Think of every desk as a unit arrow on a circle. R is the length of their average arrow and Ψ is its direction. R = 0 means the arrows point everywhere and cancel; R = 1 means every desk is identical. R is the measure of herding.

Substituting (7) into (6) makes each desk decouple from all the others and talk only to the mean field:

(8)dθi/dt = ωi + K R sin(Ψ − θi) This is the same shape as equation (3), with the crowd playing the role of the single partner and KR as the effective pull. So desk i locks when |ωi − Ω| ≤ KR, exactly inequality (5) again.

But R is itself built from the desks, so it must be consistent with what the desks do. Working out that self-consistency gives the classic result: below a critical coupling the only solution is R = 0, and above it R grows continuously from zero.

(9)Kc = 2 / (π g(0)) For a standard-normal g, g(0) = 1/√(2π) = 0.3989, giving Kc = 1.596. Wider frequency spread means smaller g(0), which means a larger Kc: more diversity is harder to synchronise, as it should be.
A scaling law worth remembering

Equation (6) is unchanged if you multiply both ω and K by the same number and slow time down to match. So only the ratio K/Δω is physical. This is why comparing two frequency distributions is meaningless unless you standardise them first, and why the safe-halt result in step 14 comes out proportional to 1/Δω.

Step 4

Why that is not a market yet

Equation (6) is elegant and wrong for our purpose, in four specific ways. The rest of the model is the repair of each one.

  1. Everybody sees everybody. The (K/N)Σj runs over all desks. Real traders watch a handful of others. Fixed in step 5 by putting it on a network.
  2. The transition is smooth. R rises continuously from zero at Kc. Markets do not drift gently into crashes, they snap. And a smooth transition has no memory: reverse the coupling and you retrace the same curve, so a crash would heal the moment its cause was removed. Fixed in steps 11 and 9.
  3. There is no price. Phases are not money. Fixed in step 11.
  4. Nothing ever surprises it. No news, no shocks, no randomness. Fixed in steps 15 and 12.

Step 5

Put the desks on a network

Replace "everyone" with an observation network. Aij = 1 if desk i watches desk j's order flow, 0 otherwise. The number of desks i watches is its degree ki.

(10)dθi/dt = ωi + (λ/⟨k⟩) Σj Aij sin(θj − θi) Dividing by the mean degree ⟨k⟩ rather than N keeps the λ axis comparable across networks with different densities. It is a pure rescaling, so it changes no physics, but the obvious alternative of dividing by ki instead is not, and it destroys the crash. Step 6 does A properly: storage, all three normalisations, and every graph used here. Step 7 answers the question this equation raises and the rest of the page walks past: where would a real A come from?

Redoing the mean-field calculation on a network (each desk now feels a field proportional to its own degree) gives a threshold that depends on structure through κ alone:

(11)λc = 2 / (π g(0) κ) Ichinomiya 2004; Restrepo, Ott & Hunt 2005. Heterogeneity lowers the threshold: hubs feel a much stronger field than average and lock first, seeding the rest.
A falsifiable claim, not a description

Clustering, assortativity and path length do not appear in (11). If they mattered, this formula would fail on a highly clustered network. That is a prediction you can test, and the sweep in the repository does test it.

Where this still breaks

The transition is still continuous. We moved the threshold; we did not change its character. Heterogeneity alone does not produce a crash.

Step 6

The connectivity matrix, in full: what A is, how it is stored, and the one choice inside it that changes the physics

Step 5 introduced Aij in half a sentence and moved on. It deserves a chapter, because it is the only object in the model that is neither a state variable nor a number you tune: it is a structure you have to supply from somewhere, and step 19 shows that the headline results of this whole project depend on which structure you supply.

The definition, with all four conventions spelled out

A is an N × N matrix of zeros and ones. Aij = 1 means desk i can see desk j's order flow. Four conventions are baked in, each of which is an assumption you could drop:

conventionmeaningassumption it encodes
binaryentries are 0 or 1, never 0.3#10: no trust weighting, no size weighting. Watching Citadel counts the same as watching a two-person shop
symmetric, Aij = Ajiwatching is mutual#9 LB: plainly false. Everyone watches the large players; the large players do not watch back
zero diagonala desk does not watch itselfharmless: the term would be sin(θi − θi) = 0 anyway, but a self-loop would inflate ki and quietly dilute ri, so make_network strips them
staticA never changes during a run#14: no rewiring in a crisis. Tested in step 24 as a co-evolving network, item A11, where rewiring toward desks that agree made things worse (R fell 0.926 to 0.712)

A node is a desk, not a stock. N = 500 means five hundred trading strategies, not five hundred tickers. The per-asset price mapping in step 11 quietly treats the same node as both, which is assumption 29 in the audit of step 23, and one of the two things step 16 blames for the ρ̄ ≡ 0 disaster.

How it is actually stored, and why ri costs nothing extra

Nobody builds the N × N matrix. With ⟨k⟩ = 12 and N = 500 it would be 250,000 entries of which 6,000 are non-zero. The code keeps a directed edge list: two integer arrays ei, ej listing each edge in both directions, so degree is just np.bincount(ei).

Then the whole coupling, and the herding gain with it, comes from exactly two sums:

(10a) ci = Σj Aij cosθj     si = Σj Aij sinθj Two bincount calls over the edge list. This is O(E), not O(N²).
(10b) Σj Aij sin(θj − θi) = si cosθi − ci sinθi
ri = √(ci² + si²) / ki The coupling is the imaginary part of the neighbour sum rotated by −θi; ri is its modulus. Both fall out of the same two numbers, which is why the herding term in step 9 costs nothing computationally. It also shows why sin is the only workable choice, which step 13 takes up properly.

Why ⟨k⟩ = 12, and the number that explains the 10% gap

⟨k⟩ = 12 is sparse enough to be a network rather than a crowd, and dense enough that the annealed mean field of equation (11), which assumes a desk sees a representative sample of the market, is not absurd. But sparseness leaves a fingerprint, and it is worth knowing its size.

Take a desk whose neighbours are completely unaligned, phases uniform and independent. The sum Σjeiθj is then a random walk of ki unit steps, so its length is of order √ki, not zero:

(10d)ri ≈ 0.886 / √ki   ≈ 0.26 at ki = 12     R ≈ 0.886 / √N ≈ 0.040 at N = 500 The mean resultant length of k random unit vectors is √(πk)/2, hence the 0.886. This is the number to know before you read any sweep.
What that number explains

1. R is never 0. Every forward branch in this repository sits at R ≈ 0.040 in the incoherent phase, and that is exactly 0.886/√500. It is the finite-size floor, not a weak signal. If you change N, the floor moves, and a "response" of 0.04 is no response.

2. The herding gain never fully switches off. Mean-field theory sets ri → 0 at incoherence, which would make the effective coupling λri vanish and R = 0 absolutely stable at every λ. On a sparse graph ri bottoms out near 0.26 instead, so there is always a residual pull, and fluctuations can light the fire. That is why λf is a measured, N-dependent and noise-dependent quantity rather than a clean theoretical one, while λb, which is a genuine fold in the equations, matches the closed form to about 10% (measured 1.116 at ⟨k⟩ = 12).

Where this leaves us

All of the above tells you how to use an A. It says nothing about where a real A would come from, and every graph in the table is invented. That is assumption 15, it is load-bearing, and step 7 is the honest answer to it.

Step 7

Where a real connectivity matrix would come from

Aij is supposed to mean "desk i watches desk j's order flow", and no one publishes that matrix. The quantity is private by construction rather than merely expensive, so every real A is an estimate, and the choice of estimator has consequences. That is why step 19 builds two different ones and reports both.

The criticism, stated properly

The objection is: this is Kuramoto oscillators dropped onto an invented graph, and the graph is supposed to be a connectivity matrix between real trading desks. That is accurate. Every crash result in this repository was measured on a synthetic Erdős-Rényi graph at ⟨k⟩ = 12, with Barabási-Albert and configuration-model scale-free graphs as comparisons. None of them was estimated from anything.

It is logged as assumption 15, it is marked LB load-bearing, and it is not a cosmetic complaint. Step 19 tests it and the answer is bad: on a network estimated from real returns the bistable window, which steps 16 and 14 depend on entirely, collapses by a factor of four and its width goes to zero. So the criticism is not "your graph is unrealistic", it is "your central result may be an artifact of your graph". That is the strongest single objection to the whole project, and it comes from inside it.

Two separate repairs are needed and they should not be confused. (i) Use a network estimated from data rather than generated: tiers 1 to 4 below. (ii) Make the nodes the right kind of object. A returns network has stocks as nodes; the model's Aij is about desks. Only tiers 2 and 4 fix that, and it is assumption 29. Tier 1 fixes (i) but not (ii), which is worth knowing before presenting it as the answer.

Five families of data can stand in for it. They are ordered by how close they get to the thing the model actually needs, which is also roughly the order of increasing cost and decreasing availability.

Tier 1. Returns, thresholded. Free, and what this repository already does

Take the price history of every constituent, compute log returns, and connect the pairs that move together. research/empirical/fetch_markets.py pulls this from Yahoo Finance through the yfinance package: 23 markets, about 2,900 constituent stocks, daily closes from 2000 to 2026, no key, no cost. research/empirical/e5_real_network.py then builds two networks from it. The recipe, exactly:

(10e) X = standardised log returns, T × n     C = XTX / T
L = X[1:−1]TX[2:] / (T−1)     LL = L − LT corr uses |C|: plain contemporaneous co-movement. leadlag uses |L − LT|, the antisymmetric part of the one-day-lagged cross-correlation, which keeps the pair i and j when i leads j more strongly than j leads i.

Then a threshold. Both matrices are dense, so you keep only the strongest ⟨k⟩n/2 entries, with the cut chosen so the mean degree comes out at 12, matching the synthetic benchmark. Degree is then held fixed by construction and only structure differs, which is what makes the comparison in step 19 a controlled one. Finally symmetrise and drop self-loops.

Better estimators than a hard threshold, all standard and all worth trying, since the threshold is the weakest link in the chain:

  • Partial correlation or the precision matrix: removes the market factor, so you stop connecting two stocks merely because both follow the index.
  • Graphical lasso: gives a sparse graph directly from an L1 penalty, so sparsity is estimated rather than imposed by a cut you chose.
  • Minimum spanning tree (Mantegna 1999) and the planar maximally filtered graph (Tumminello et al. 2005): filters with no free threshold at all.
  • Granger causality networks (Billio et al. 2012) and the Diebold-Yilmaz spillover index: directed by construction, which fits "who watches whom" far better than a correlation.
  • Transfer entropy or tail-dependence networks, if you care about the crash regime specifically rather than the average one.
A circularity worth naming

A return-correlation network is an outcome. The model's A is supposed to be an input that generates co-movement. Estimating A from co-movement and then asking whether the model reproduces co-movement is close to circular, and the high clustering of the corr network is partly an artifact of thresholding a single-factor covariance matrix: if everything loads on the market, the strongest pairs form a dense blob with many triangles.

The lead-lag network is the better of the two for precisely this reason: it uses the time asymmetry, which a common factor does not produce. Note its clustering, 0.077, is a tenth of the corr network's, and closer to the synthetic value. Any serious version of step 19 should sweep the threshold and report both networks, which is exactly the caveat that step records.

The construction, which is the part worth getting right:

  1. Build the bipartite matrix H, managers × stocks, entry = portfolio weight. This is already the honest structure that the bipartite trader-by-asset extension, item A13 in step 24, proposed for assumption 29.
  2. Project it to a manager-manager graph. The naive projection, cosine similarity of holdings, measures who owns the same things, which is exposure overlap rather than imitation.
  3. The projection you actually want uses changes: correlate ΔH across quarters, so an edge means "when i bought, j bought too". That is a behavioural link, which is what Aij claims to be. Lagging it by one quarter makes it directed, and gives you a real lead-lag among traders.
  4. Threshold to ⟨k⟩ = 12 exactly as in tier 1, so the comparison against the synthetic benchmark stays controlled, then report κ, C and assortativity.
The cheapest useful next step

It is free, it is scriptable against EDGAR in an afternoon, and it is the only tier that gives a network whose nodes are the same kind of object as the model's nodes. It would address assumption 15 and assumption 29 at the same time, which no result in this repository currently does.

The limitation is severe: 13F is quarterly. The 2010 flash crash lasted 36 minutes. That is seven orders of magnitude, and it is assumption 41 wearing a different hat. A 13F network is a credible estimate of the market's structure and tells you nothing about the timing of anything. Use it to get C and κ right; do not use it to date a crash.

Tier 3. Trade and order data. Paid, and the only tier at the model's own timescale

Message-level feeds record every order, modification and execution with microsecond timestamps. The participant is usually anonymous, but orders carry IDs, so sequences of orders can be clustered into strategies, and lead-lag between strategy clusters can then be measured at the millisecond scale. That is as close to the true A as anyone outside a regulator gets.

Step 8

What actually makes a transition abrupt

A crash is a first-order transition: R jumps discontinuously, and the forward and backward paths differ, which is hysteresis. Three known mechanisms produce this. They are genuinely different and it matters which you pick.

Route A: correlate frequency with degree

Set ωi ∝ ki. Because the field a desk feels also scales with ki, the desks that are hardest to entrain are exactly the ones with the most pull. Nobody can lock alone, so nothing locks until everything locks at once (Gómez-Gardeñes et al. 2011).

Measured here, and sharper than the textbook version

The sweep in this repository varied the rank correlation c between ω and k while controlling the shape of g(ω) separately. Result: a Gaussian frequency distribution never produces a jump at any correlation (ΔR ≈ 0.08 flat from c = −1 to +1). The jump appears only at |c| = 1 and only when g(ω) has a tail as heavy as the degree distribution (ΔR = 0.578 with a degree-matched marginal, 0.498 with a Lorentzian). Even c = 0.75 gives almost nothing.

So the right statement is not "frequency-degree correlation causes explosive synchronisation". It is: the tail of g(ω) must match the tail of P(k), and a rank correlation is not enough.

Route B: change the shape of g(ω)

Even with all-to-all coupling and no network at all, a uniform frequency distribution makes the Kuramoto transition first-order, with R jumping to exactly π/4 ≈ 0.785 (Pazó 2005). A bimodal distribution does something similar near λ = 2ω0 (Martens et al. 2009). Note g(0) = 0 for a bimodal law, so formula (11) does not even apply.

Route C: make the coupling adaptive

This is the one that fits a market, and it is step 9.

Step 9

The herding term, which is where the crash comes from

Route A needs desks' trading horizons to be proportional to their connectivity, which is an odd thing to assert about a market. Route C assumes something far more natural: you copy harder when the people you are watching already agree with each other.

Measure that agreement locally. For desk i, take the average arrow of just its neighbours:

(12)ri = |Σj Aij eiθj| / ki ri = 0 when i's neighbours are split evenly, ri = 1 when they are unanimous. It is a purely local quantity: desk i can actually observe it.

Now multiply the coupling by it (Zhang, Boccaletti, Guan & Liu 2015):

(13)dθi/dt = ωi + (λ/⟨k⟩) ri Σj Aij sin(θj − θi)
Why this one term changes everything

It is positive feedback. More agreement raises ri, which raises the effective coupling, which produces more agreement. In the plain model (10) the pull is linear in the crowd's coherence; here it is roughly quadratic, and that is enough to turn a smooth transition into a jump with a bistable region.

The mean-field version makes the mechanism explicit. Writing u = λR² for the effective locking strength:

(14)R = H(u) = u ∫−π/2π/2 cos²φ · g(u sin φ) dφ,     λ(u) = u / H(u)² λ(u) diverges as u → 0 and grows again for large u, so it has a minimum. That minimum is a saddle-node bifurcation, and it is the recovery threshold λb.

The consequence is the single most important structure in the model:

λ < λbonly R ≈ 0 is stable calm. A shock causes a flash crash that heals by itself
λb < λ < λf two stable states bistable. The same shock can leave the market permanently crashed

Measured on the configuration the simulation ships with (ER, ⟨k⟩ = 12, f = 1, σ = 0.3): λb = 3.1, λf = 4.1, hysteresis gap 0.80. You can reproduce this live with the auto-ramp button.

Step 10 is the chapter this table deserves: what the relationship between λ and R actually is, why the market responds to λR rather than to λ, the measured forward and backward branches, and the nine reasons a sweep can look completely flat.

And for Lorentzian frequencies it is exactly solvable

The classic Kuramoto result R = √(1 − Kc/K) with Kc = 2γ enters the mean field as K·R. Here the gain is αi = ri, so it enters as λR², i.e. Keff = λR. Substituting gives a cubic,

λR³ − λR + 2γ = 0

whose fold (double root, 3λR² = λ) sits at R* = 1/√3, giving a closed form for the recovery threshold: λb = 3√3 γ ≈ 5.196γ, with R* = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577 at the fold. Verified numerically: at λ = 5.2, just above the fold, the two roots are 0.5901 and 0.5645, converging on 0.5774 as they must. This replaces the numerical saddle-node search that step 9's mean field used, which was about 10% off.

Hysteresis is the point

A crash persists not because the shock persists, but because the market has moved onto a different stable branch. Whether a shock heals or sticks is a property of where λ sits, not of how big the shock was.

Step 10

Turning λ, and watching R answer

Step 9 quoted λb = 3.1 and λf = 4.1 as if the connection between the coupling and the synchronisation parameter were obvious. It is not obvious at all, and the question deserves a chapter of its own: when I raise the herding coupling λ, why does the synchronisation parameter R go up?

The short version is that λ sets the width of a capture window in frequency space, R measures how much of the population that window has swallowed, and because the width of the window is itself proportional to R, the two quantities chase each other. The rest of this chapter is that sentence, slowly.

What the coupling physically does to one desk

Switch the coupling off and the desks are hopeless: each one rotates at its own rate ωi, they drift apart within a few cycles, and their arrows end up spread uniformly around the circle so that the average arrow has essentially no length. That is R ≈ 0, and it is the natural state of the market. Diversity of trading horizons, on its own, guarantees incoherence.

Now switch the coupling on. Desk i is pulled toward the average direction of the desks it watches, and from step 3 the strength of that pull is

(14a)hi = (λ/⟨k⟩) · ki · R λ is the coupling you set, ki is how many desks i watches, and R is how aligned the market already is. All three multiply.

The pull competes against the desk's own stubbornness, which is how far its natural pace sits from the pace of the herd, |ωi − Ω|. Whichever is larger wins, and the outcome is binary:

(14b) |ωi − Ω| ≤ hi  ⇒  captured,   sin(θi − Ψ) = (ωi − Ω) / hi
|ωi − Ω| > hi  ⇒  drifting,   contributes almost nothing to R A captured desk stops rotating relative to the herd and parks at a fixed angular offset from it. A drifting desk keeps going round, so over any reasonable window its arrow points every which way and averages out.

Read equation (14b) as a window. It says there is an interval of natural frequencies, centred on the herd's pace Ω and of half-width hi, and every desk whose ωi falls inside that interval gets locked into the herd. Desks outside it escape. hi is the width of the net, and λ is what you use to widen it.

Why widening the net raises R, twice over

Raise λ and hi grows in direct proportion. Two things then happen at once, and both of them push R up.

The first is the obvious one. A wider window contains more of the frequency distribution g(ω), so desks that were drifting a moment ago are now captured. Each newly captured desk stops cancelling out and starts adding its arrow to the average, in roughly the same direction as everyone else's. The count of aligned arrows goes up.

The second is easier to miss and matters just as much. Look at the offset a captured desk settles at: sin(θi − Ψ) = (ωi − Ω)/hi. The numerator is fixed, a property of the desk. The denominator is hi, which you just increased. So every desk that was already captured now sits at a smaller angle from the herd's direction. Double hi and a desk that used to lean 60° away from the crowd leans about 26°. Its arrow contributes cos of that offset to the average, so the contribution rises.

More desks in the net, and each one held closer to the centre of it. Summing over both effects gives the length of the average arrow, which is R by definition:

(14c)R = ∫captured cos(θ(ω) − Ψ) · g(ω) dω The integral runs only over the captured band, which is the part λ controls, and the cosine inside it is how tightly each captured desk is held, which λ also controls. That is the whole answer to the question. Everything below is the consequence of R appearing on both sides of it.

The part that makes this interesting: the net widens itself

Equation (14a) contains R. Equation (14c) produces R. So the two cannot be solved one after the other, only together, and that changes the character of the answer completely.

Follow what happens after a small increase in λ. The window widens slightly, so a few extra desks are captured, so R rises slightly. But hi is proportional to R, so the window widens again on its own, without any further help from you, capturing more desks, raising R again. The question is whether that cascade settles or runs away.

Below the critical coupling it settles immediately, and settles at nothing: each round of the cascade is weaker than the one before, the sum converges to zero, and the only self-consistent answer is R = 0. Above the critical coupling each round is stronger than the last until the supply of easily captured desks runs out, and the cascade stops at some R > 0. In plain Kuramoto this crossover happens smoothly, at Kc = 2/(πg(0)) from equation (9), and R grows continuously from zero. This is the reason R responds to λ at all, and the reason the response is a threshold rather than a proportionality.

Notice also what the feedback implies about the calm state. At R ≈ 0 the pull hi is also ≈ 0 no matter how large λ is, because the two are multiplied. A market with no agreement in it is nearly immune to the coupling. That is why turning λ up in the incoherent phase looks like it does nothing at all, and it is not a bug in the simulation. It is the mechanism.

With the herding term, the feedback is stronger than linear

Step 9 multiplied the coupling by the local agreement, αi = ri. In mean field ri → R, so the capture width acquires a second factor of R:

(14d)hi ∝ λR²   ⇒   Keff = λR A desk does not feel λ. It feels λ multiplied by how much agreement already exists. In the incoherent state, where R ≈ 0.04, raising λ from 2 to 4 raises the effective coupling from 0.08 to 0.16, which is nowhere near enough to capture anybody.

Now the cascade has a sharper character. Each round of capture raises R, which widens the window quadratically rather than linearly, which captures proportionally more desks than before. Weak agreement is self-suppressing and strong agreement is self-amplifying, so there is no stable middle. The market either stays incoherent or goes all the way over, and the transition becomes a jump.

Two honest caveats about those thresholds

λf and λb are defined in the code as the first grid point at which R crosses 0.5 (transition_points in model.py). The level 0.5 is arbitrary, which is assumption 37, and the grid here is 0.125 wide, so neither number is resolved better than that.

The two are also not equally trustworthy. λb is a fold in the equations and it matches the closed form of equation (14f) to about ten per cent. λf is wherever a fluctuation happened to be large enough to carry the system over the middle branch, so it depends on N, on σ, on how fast the sweep moved and on the random seed. It should not be quoted as a constant of the model.

Step 11

From phases to prices, plus noise

Aggregate the order flow, and let the imbalance move the price:

(15)Q = (1/N) Σi sin θi = R sin Ψ

Equation (15) is worth staring at. A crash needs R high and Ψ near −90°. R high with Ψ near +90° is a melt-up. The model produces both, symmetrically, which is already a hint that something is missing, since real markets fall faster than they rise.

Step 12

Shocks, and why some crashes stick

A shock is a common sell signal pushing a chosen subset of desks toward θ = −π/2:

(18)… + ε(t) si sin(−π/2 − θi) ε is the shock strength and si marks which desks are hit. The fraction hit is φ. This is the coupling term again with one phantom neighbour pinned at −π/2; step 13 explains why the target is that angle and not a free parameter, why it is a sine rather than a constant push, and the one artifact the form introduces.

Step 13

Why every term is a sine, why the shock aims at −π/2, and which of these choices are free

Three sines have now been introduced without justification: q = sinθ in equation (2), sin(θj − θi) in the coupling, and sin(−π/2 − θi) in the shock. They are not the same kind of choice. One is pure convention, one is forced by a theorem, and one follows from the first two. Knowing which is which tells you what you are allowed to change.

1. q = sinθ is a convention, and cos would do equally well

Order flow has to be a bounded, 2π-periodic function of the phase that averages to zero over a full cycle, so a desk neither accumulates nor liquidates forever. sin and cos both satisfy all three. Choosing sin fixes nothing but the dictionary:

θ0+π/2π−π/2
q = sinθ0, flat+1, peak buying0, flat−1, peak selling

Had you picked q = cosθ, peak selling would sit at θ = π instead, the constant SELL in model.py would be np.pi, and every phase-histogram axis label in the repository would shift by 90°. No result would change. This is genuinely arbitrary.

What is not arbitrary is that q contains only the first harmonic. One buy phase and one sell phase per revolution, smoothly. A desk that bought twice per cycle would need a sin2θ term. That is assumption 8, and it also quietly imposes assumption 3: every desk's maximum size is 1, so a pension fund and a two-person shop contribute equally to Q, which is flatly false of real order flow and marked load-bearing for that reason.

2. The coupling must be a sine, and cosine coupling provably cannot synchronise anything

Start from what the coupling is allowed to depend on. There is no absolute zero of phase, so rotating every desk by the same angle must change nothing. The interaction can therefore only be a function of the difference, H(θj − θi), and it must be 2π-periodic. Every such function has a Fourier series:

(18a)H(Δ) = Σn≥1 [ an cos nΔ + bn sin nΔ ] Now ask what each piece does to the difference between two desks, which is the only thing that matters for locking.

Take the two-desk system of step 2 with cosine coupling instead of sine, and subtract, exactly as in equation (4):

(18b) dφ/dt = Δω + K[ cos(−φ) − cos(φ) ] = Δω + 0 = Δω Because cosine is even, cos(−φ) = cos(φ) and the two coupling terms cancel identically. The phase difference obeys the same equation it would with no coupling at all. Two desks coupled by a cosine never lock, at any K, for any frequencies.

The result generalises: only the odd part of H can align phases. The even part is not literally inert for N desks, it adds KΣjcos(θj − θi) to desk i's instantaneous rate, so desks that agree with their neighbours speed up, but it cannot pull them together. Drop the even harmonics, keep the lowest odd one, and you have sin(θj − θi). Kuramoto's model is the first term of the only series that could have worked.

The sine also has exactly the properties the story needs, which is a good check rather than a coincidence:

  • Zero at alignment. A desk that already agrees with its neighbour feels no pull. Copying stops when there is nothing left to copy.
  • Odd, so it is a genuine attraction. If j is ahead, i speeds up; if j is behind, i slows down. Swapping the roles flips the sign.
  • Maximal at ±90°, where the disagreement is largest but still resolvable.
  • Zero at antiphase (Δ = π), and unstable there: a pair exactly opposed feels nothing, but the tiniest perturbation collapses it. Perfect disagreement is a knife edge.
The second reason, which matters for the implementation

sin(θj − θi) = Im[ eiθj e−iθi ], so the sum over neighbours factorises into something that depends only on j, summed once, times something that depends only on i. That is precisely what lets the order parameter of equation (7) exist, what turns N coupled equations into one desk talking to a mean field in equation (8), and what makes the closed forms of step 21 possible at all. It is also why the code needs only two bincount sums per step, equation (10a), and why ri comes out of the same two numbers for free. Any coupling that is not sinusoidal destroys the mean-field reduction and the O(E) implementation together.

3. The shock: a phantom neighbour pinned at maximal selling

Now equation (18) reads as one object rather than three symbols:

(18c)… + ε(t) · si · sin(θtgt − θi),     θtgt = −π/2 This is the coupling term again, with a single imaginary neighbour whose phase is fixed at θtgt and whose edge weight is ε. No new mathematics, and it inherits every property listed above.

Why −π/2 and not something else. The shock means "sell now", and "selling" is defined by equation (2): q = sinθ, which reaches −1 at θ = −π/2 and nowhere else. So the target is not a free parameter, it is forced by the convention in part 1 of this chapter. It appears in the code as a single constant, SELL = -np.pi/2. If you switched to q = cosθ, the correct target would become π. Nothing physical distinguishes the two.

Why a sine here too, rather than a constant push. Three properties that a constant does not have:

  • It vanishes when the desk arrives. A sell signal cannot push a desk that is already selling as hard as it can. A constant torque −ε would spin desks straight past the target and keep going round forever, so the market would never settle into a crash, it would tumble.
  • It takes the short way round. A desk at θ = 0 is turned down toward −π/2; a desk at θ = π is turned up toward it. The signal always chooses the nearer route, which is what "everyone rushes to sell" should mean.
  • It competes on equal terms with the herding term, because it has the same functional form. That is what makes the comparison meaningful: a shock of strength ε against a crowd of strength λriki/⟨k⟩, one number against another, in the same units.
One artifact of this form

sin(θtgt − θi) vanishes at two places: at θi = θtgt = −π/2, which is the point of the whole thing, and also at θi = θtgt + π = +π/2, peak buying. A desk sitting exactly at maximal buying feels no shock at all.

It is an unstable point, so it is measure-zero and any noise dislodges it, but the consequence is real and visible: the desks slowest to respond to a sell shock are the ones currently buying hardest, which is the opposite of what you would expect from a market. It is a property of the sinusoidal form, not of traders. The same artifact exists in the coupling at antiphase, and it is one concrete reason to care about the higher harmonics that assumption 8 discards.

Why a mask si and a fraction, rather than hitting everyone. Crashes start somewhere. Making the target selectable is what allows the result in step 12 that who you hit matters as much as how hard: because the local field scales with degree, hitting 10 to 15% of desks chosen as hubs reliably flips the market, while the same number of peripheral desks does almost nothing. With a uniform shock that question cannot even be asked. (The fraction hit is written φ, which collides with the phase difference φ of equation (4). See step 0.)

Why ε is a function of time. It lets the same machinery express a pulse, a step or a ramp, and the ramp matters: S7 shows the transition depends on the rate at which the driver changes, not only its final level, which is rate-dependent tipping.

Step 14

Circuit breakers as desynchronisation

If a crash is a synchronised state, a trading halt is an attempt to desynchronise. Cut the coupling and every desk free-runs at its own ω, so the cluster spreads out at a rate set by the diversity of frequencies:

(19)Rhalt(t) = |∫ g(ω) ei[arcsin(ω/u) + ωt] dω| · e−σ²t/2

Trading may resume safely once the herd has spread out past the basin boundary, which gives a minimum safe halt length:

(20)τ*(λ) = min{ τ : Rhalt(τ) < Ru(λ) }   →   τ* = f(λ/λb) / Δω The halt must be longer the more herded the market is, and shorter the more diverse its trading horizons are. Too short and it re-crashes on resumption.
Honest accuracy

This theory reproduces the trend but sits 16 to 44% below the simulated boundary, and halts largely stop working near λf, where noise re-triggers the crash regardless. This is the most novel part of the repository and also the least precise.

Step 15

Now test it on real markets

23 markets, about 2,900 constituent stocks, daily data 2000 to 2026, 324 crash onsets. Two predictions were tested, and both failed.

E1: no early warning

Against random dates several indicators look predictive. Against the fair comparison, ordinary market peaks not followed by a crash, every indicator's confidence interval touches 0.5. Best AUC 0.571. Co-movement measures are flat until the peak and rise only after the fall begins: they react to crashes, they do not anticipate them.

E2: no co-movement hysteresis

The model says co-movement should be higher coming out of a crash than going in, at the same volatility. Measured median gap: 0.0006 to 0.0031, with confidence intervals containing zero. A planted +0.03 effect was recovered, so the test had power to detect 0.03.

E6: the 2010 flash crash, finally, at 1-minute resolution

A free 1-minute feed covering 2002 onward (HF Data Library, CC BY 4.0) reaches 2010-05-06. Across 32 large caps, co-movement rose +0.137 in the 92 minutes BEFORE 14:32 and then fell slightly during the crash itself. On daily data E1 found the reverse: flat until the peak, rising only afterwards.

The null cuts this down, though. Running the same 13:00 to 14:32 window on every day that week: the crash day has the largest afternoon rise (+0.141), but 2010-05-05 reaches +0.115 and the crash day's absolute level is not even the week's highest (2010-05-07, the aftermath, is). With one crash and four control days, and the whole week inside the European debt crisis, this cannot separate a precursor from ordinary intraday variation. Suggestive, not established.

E7: the timescale objection, settled at the model's own timescale

This was the strongest objection to everything above: the model is intraday, the 2010 flash crash lasted 36 minutes, and these tests used daily closes. So the whole study was redone on 2,319,682 one-minute bars spanning 2002 to 2026, 31 intraday crash events, with E1's own matched null.

The raw result looks spectacular: pre-event co-movement 0.458 before crashes against 0.228 before ordinary peaks, AUC 0.911 [0.843, 0.969], against E1's 0.542 on daily data. Taken at face value, E1's null was a resolution artifact.

It is not. Crashes happen in high-volatility regimes and co-movement rises with volatility, so the unmatched comparison scores well purely as a volatility proxy. Pre-event volatility is 4.94× higher before crashes. Pairing each crash with the nearest-volatility ordinary peak, exactly as E1 did on daily data:

AUC 0.897  →  0.526, CI [0.340, 0.712]

Once volatility is held fixed, co-movement carries no skill at minute resolution either. The same confound structure appears at both timescales. E1's daily null was not a resolution artifact, and E1's conclusion survives the test it was most vulnerable to.

Caveats: 21 crashes survive the pre-window requirement, the matched interval is correspondingly wide, and the cross-section is 23 large caps rather than a full index. A larger ticker set would tighten the interval, but 0.526 is not close to meaningful skill.

But E2 flagged a caveat about itself, and that caveat turns out to be the whole story.

Step 16

The observable gap: the model could not possibly have passed

In the price mapping, each asset's return was driven by its own desk's order flow plus independent noise. In a locked crash every qi is pinned near a constant, so each return is a constant plus independent noise, so the mean pairwise correlation is zero. Not small: zero, as an identity, at every value of R.

What that means

E2 tested a prediction the model never made. Meanwhile real markets are unambiguous: correlation rises from calm to crash in 23 markets out of 23, median +0.127 (0.229 → 0.389). The model produced 0.000. It was failing the most basic co-movement fact before hysteresis was even at issue.

The repair is one parameter: the common market mode σc from equation (17). With it, each asset loads on the shared shock with weight cos θi, and those weights are aligned precisely when the phases are aligned. Writing a = βσc and v for the idiosyncratic return variance:

(21) ui = a cosθi / √(a²cos²θi + v),    ρ̄ = ⟨uiuj⟩ = ⟨u⟩²
weak-mode limit:   ρ̄ ≈ (a²/v) R² cos²Ψ Since ⟨cosθ⟩ = R cosΨ. So ρ̄ ∝ R²: synchrony finally becomes visible in the observable that E2 actually measures.

Simulating this confirms the mechanism and the formula (measured crash co-movement rises 0.0001 to 0.0955 as σc goes 0 to 0.8, tracked by ⟨u⟩² within about a factor of two). Then comes the decisive number:

E2 had no power

Recomputing E2's own gap statistic on the repaired model gives 0.0002 to 0.0028 at every σc. E2's detection bound was 0.013. Even with the mapping fixed, the model predicts an effect roughly four times smaller than the smallest one E2 could have seen.

So E2's null result does not falsify the model's hysteresis. It is a negative result about the test, not about the model.

But one channel is still not enough

σc cannot produce a calm-period baseline at all (0.0006 against a real 0.229). The obvious alternative, a common shock in the prices, has the opposite failure: it matches the level but produces no crash rise (+0.008 against a real +0.127). A calibrated model needs both channels. Neither alone fits.

Step 18

Can you actually measure the coupling? No, and the reason is the model itself

Everything operational in steps 16 and 14 is phrased as where λ sits relative to λb. None of it can be used unless λ can be measured in a real market. Equation (21) seems to offer exactly that: measure co-movement, invert to get R, then invert the model's own equilibrium curve to get λ.

(23)ρ̄ → R̂ = Rmax √(ρ̄/ρ̄max) → λ̂ = Req−1(R̂)

Applied to 27,723 market-days across 23 markets, it fails, for two independent reasons that are worth more than the method would have been.

1. You cannot invert a step function

Req(λ) jumps from R = 0.137 to R = 0.899 across a λ window only 0.167 wide. That is not a numerical problem, it is the definition of a first-order transition. Every measured R in between is consistent with that whole window, and 97.2% of real market-days land inside it. The estimate ends up with an inter-quartile range of 0.04.

The property that makes explosive synchronization interesting is exactly the property that makes its control parameter unidentifiable from its order parameter.

2. Even a perfect inversion could not help

λ̂ is a monotone transform of ρ̄, and AUC is invariant under monotone transforms. So λ̂ must score identically to raw mean correlation on any ranking test. No reparametrisation of an indicator can ever beat that indicator.

This kills what had been the headline recommendation of step 21. It is kept here because a negative identifiability result is a real constraint on the whole programme: any first-order synchronization model of markets inherits it.

Step 25

What this turned into, and what was explored on the way

The opening idea was narrow: represent a trading desk as a phase oscillator, let desks imitate the desks they watch, and see whether the resulting market has a synchronisation transition sharp enough to look like a crash. Steps 1 to 5 got as far as Kuramoto oscillators on a network, which has a transition but a smooth one, and a smooth transition is useless here because it has no memory: remove the cause and the crash heals instantly, which is not what markets do.

Step 8 laid out the three known ways to make such a transition abrupt, and step 9 chose the one that reads like a market rather than a coincidence: a desk copies harder when the desks it watches already agree with each other. That single multiplication, αi = ri, produced everything the project was built on afterwards. The transition became a jump, a bistable window opened between λb and λf, and inside that window the same shock either heals or becomes permanent depending only on where the coupling sits. Steps 11 to 14 built on it: a price from the order imbalance, a shock that can be aimed at hubs or at the periphery, and a theory of trading halts as forced desynchronisation whose central quantity is the minimum safe halt length.

At that point the model was internally coherent and had produced a genuinely new-looking piece of theory. Everything after step 15 is the attempt to check it against data, and that is where the story changes direction.

Where it ended up

Two things survive as positive contributions, both analytic and both in step 22. The first is a closed form covering a whole family of models at once: generalising the herding gain to αi = rip gives λb(p) = γ(p+2)((p+2)/p)p/2, which reduces to the classical Kuramoto threshold 2γ as p goes to zero, to 3√3 γ for the pairwise herding model, and to 8γ for the three-body hypergraph interaction that ri2 turns out to be. The ratio between prediction and simulation stays flat at about 1.105 while the prediction itself moves by a factor of 2.1, which is the evidence that the formula has the right shape rather than the right value at one point. The second is the critical clustering C* ≈ 0.35, which puts a number on an effect the literature had only described qualitatively, and which happens to explain why the model fails on real networks.

Everything else is either a reproduction or a negative result, and the negative results are the more useful half. As a model of markets this one does not work: it cannot anticipate a crash out of sample, plain volatility outperforms its own observable, the part of co-movement that is specifically synchronisation carries no predictive information, its control parameter cannot be measured even in principle, and the bistable window that its most novel results depend on is a property of an unclustered synthetic graph rather than of a market. As a study of explosive synchronisation it works well, and the honest summary of the whole project is that it is a clean piece of nonlinear dynamics that was calibrated against the wrong network and asked to do a job the mathematics forbids.

What would change that verdict is not another term in the equation. It is a connectivity matrix estimated from data in which the nodes are trading desks rather than stocks, which is step 7, and an intraday dataset in which the nodes and the timescale finally match, which is the surviving half of assumption 41.

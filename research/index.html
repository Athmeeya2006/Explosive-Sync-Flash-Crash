Bottom line

What holds up

  1. A closed-form threshold family (S13, new). With gain alpha_i = r_i^p the fold solves exactly: R*(p) = sqrt(p/(p+2)) and lambda_b(p) = gamma(p+2)((p+2)/p)^(p/2). One expression spans plain Kuramoto (p→0, 2γ), pairwise herding (p=1, 3√3γ) and the 3-body hypergraph case (p=2, 8γ). Simulated/predicted ratio is flat at 1.105 ± 0.055 across a 2.1x range of thresholds.
  2. A critical clustering (S13, new). C* ≈ 0.35: above it the bistable window closes. Synthetic ER (C = 0.030) sits far below, the real market correlation network (C = 0.731) sits 2.1x above. This explains E5 rather than merely reporting it.
  3. Exact thresholds (S11). For Lorentzian frequencies the herding model is solvable in closed form: the cubic λR³ − λR + 2γ = 0 folds at R* = 1/√3, giving λb = 3√3 γ. The residual 12% against simulation is the finite-connectivity correction, which explains the ~10% offset S2 could only measure.
  4. The first-order jump is genuine (S11). It grows with system size, 0.183 at N = 125 to 0.486 at N = 4000, rather than decaying. Not a finite-size artifact.
  5. What actually causes explosiveness (S8c). Not a frequency-degree rank correlation: with a Gaussian frequency law there is no jump at any correlation. The tail of g(ω) must match the tail of P(k), and even c = 0.75 gives almost nothing.
  6. Separability classification (A1). Kuramoto couplings are provably non-factorizable (D₁ ≡ 1, rank 2) and the herding coupling is an exact 3-body interaction, so the Hens et al. propagation theory does not cover them.
  7. Two model extensions earn their place (S10). State-dependent liquidity is the only change that produces fat tails or volatility clustering at all; inertia widens the bistable window 3.6x.

What did not hold up

  • The model's own network is wrong (E5). Real market networks are an order of magnitude more clustered than the synthetic ER graph every crash result uses, and the bistable window essentially vanishes on them (gap 0.793 → 0.17). S4 and S5, the two most novel items, both depend on that window existing. This is the most serious result here.
  • No early warning, at any timescale, and it is not usable (E1, E7, E8). On daily data no indicator beats ordinary peaks. At 1-minute resolution over 2.3M bars the raw AUC is 0.911, but volatility-matched it is 0.526 [0.340, 0.712]. Out of sample the best detector needs 77 false alarms per crash caught, and co-movement with volatility removed is the worst of three detectors. The timescale objection is answered and E1 survives it.
  • λ cannot be measured (A3). Req(λ) is a step function, so 97.2% of market-days carry no information about λ, and a monotone transform cannot beat its own indicator. The headline recommendation is withdrawn.
  • Prior work does not fully generalize (E3). Lee et al. (PNAS 2025) does not reproduce at constituent level for 2008; out of sample across 262 events only the recovery half survives, weakly (ρ = 0.13).
  • Two proposed extensions must be rejected (S10). Contrarians destroy the transition entirely; asymmetric herding gives the wrong sign on the leverage effect.
  • No co-movement hysteresis in real data (E2), though S9 shows the test lacked the power to see the model's own prediction by a factor of four.
  • The repository's original degree-normalized model has no explosive synchronization (S1).

Honest status: the analytic core is in better shape than when this started (closed-form thresholds, a genuine discontinuity, a sharper cause), while the empirical case is weaker (no early warning at any resolution, and the central bistability largely absent on real networks). The most valuable next step is deciding whether E5's collapse is the clustering gap or an artifact of how the network was built, because steps S4 and S5 stand or fall on it.

Crash-type phase diagram and shock targeting

new result

Question

For herding λ and shock size ε, is a shock absorbed, a flash crash, or a lasting crash? Does it matter which traders are hit?

Setup

ER N = 500, f = 1, σ = 0.3. Grid 20 λ × 16 ε × 4 seeds (settle 60, shock 8, relax 150). Targeting: λ = 3.51, ε = 2, shock applied to a fraction φ of traders chosen by degree, natural frequency, or at random; 10 fractions × 5 strategies × 12 seeds.

Script: s4_crash_phase_diagram.py

Model / derivation

Classes: absorbed (R never exceeds 0.5), flash (exceeds 0.5 but relaxes), persistent (R ≥ 0.5 at the end), already crashed (R ≥ 0.5 before the shock).

Results

  • The flash/persistent boundary lies between λ = 3.1 (no lasting crash for any ε) and λ = 3.4 (lasting crash in every run for ε ≥ 0.23), bracketing λb = 3.21 from S2; above the smallest shock it does not depend on ε.
  • Inside the window the second-smallest shock (ε = 0.23) already gives a lasting crash; with the smallest (ε = 0.1) the probability climbs from 0 at λ = 3.4 to 1 at λ = 3.8.
  • From λ = 3.8 some markets crash before any shock (25% of runs), rising to 100% at λ = 4.3 (noise-induced escape near λf = 3.96).
  • Hitting 10-15% of traders is enough. At φ = 0.10 the probability of a persistent crash is 0.83 (hubs), 0.67 (random), 0.50 (slow traders), 0.17 (fast traders), 0.08 (periphery); at φ = 0.15 it is 1.00 for hubs, random and slow traders, 0.67 fast, 0.58 periphery. Hubs are the most and the periphery the least dangerous target; hubs vs random is within noise (12 seeds, SE ≈ 0.14), hubs vs periphery is not.

Real data: do early-warning signals precede actual crashes?

negative result

Question

At a market peak, can any indicator tell whether a ≥10% crash (30% for crypto) follows within 30 trading days?

Setup

23 markets: US large/mid/small cap, UK 100/250, Germany, France, Spain, Switzerland, Netherlands, Italy, Sweden, Euro Stoxx 50, Japan, Hong Kong, India, Australia, Canada, Brazil, Korea, Singapore, 38 world indices (as nodes) and 40 cryptocurrencies; ≈2,900 constituents, daily 2000-2026 (current constituents: survivorship bias). Onset = true peak before the fall. Six indicators on 60-day windows using only past data. Three comparison sets; the fairest is peaks NOT followed by a crash. 95% CIs by cluster bootstrap over calendar quarters (a global crash hits many markets at once).

Script: empirical/e1_ews_all_markets.py (+ fetch_markets.py)

Model / derivation

Indicators on a trailing 60-day window (r = daily log return, i = stock, t = day): Scores at date d: level and trend = Kendall τ of the indicator against time over the preceding 250 days.

Results

  • 324 scored crash onsets in 23 markets, 69 distinct calendar quarters.

What actually makes the transition explosive

partly confirmed

Question

Topology, frequency law, frequency-degree correlation and noise are all said to drive explosive synchronization. Under one matched normalization, which of them really does?

Setup

Factorial sweep: 6 topologies (ER, BA, scale-free, Watts-Strogatz, ring, random regular) x 4 frequency laws x correlation c, all standardized to zero mean and unit scale so the coupling axis is comparable. N = 600, adiabatic sweeps. S8c repeats the correlation sweep after a design error was found in S8.

Script: s8_topology_frequency_sweep.py + s8c_correlation_corrected.py

Model / derivation

Correlation is imposed by a Gaussian copula, , which fixes the RANK correlation while forcing the marginal of . That is the error: the Gomez-Gardenes mechanism needs , i.e. proportionality, not ordering. With a Gaussian marginal the largest hub receives rather than .

Results

  • A Gaussian frequency law never produces a jump, at any correlation. ΔR stays flat at 0.08 from c = -1 to +1, with zero bistable window.
  • The jump appears only at |c| = 1 and only when the tail of g(ω) matches the tail of P(k): ΔR = 0.578 with a degree-matched marginal, 0.498 with a Lorentzian, 0.088 with a Gaussian. Even c = 0.75 gives almost nothing.
  • So the correct statement is not "frequency-degree correlation causes explosive synchronization", it is the tail of g(ω) must match the tail of P(k); a rank correlation is not sufficient.
  • No-free-parameter check against S1: predicted λf = 1.56·⟨k⟩/std(k) = 1.357, measured 1.562, ratio 1.15.
  • Noise erodes the transition monotonically but does not destroy it: ΔR 0.578 → 0.480 and the window 0.438 → 0.250 as σ goes 0 → 0.8.
  • Correction to S8 itself: the first factorial used too short a relaxation and returned negative hysteresis widths, which is unphysical. Only its ΔR values are quoted; S8c doubles the relaxation.

  • With the common mode on, crash co-movement rises 0.0001 → 0.0955 as σc goes 0 → 0.8, and the closed form ⟨u⟩² tracks it within about a factor of two.
  • The decisive number: E2's own gap statistic computed on the repaired model is 0.0002 to 0.0028 at every σc, against E2's detection bound of 0.013. Even with the mapping fixed the predicted effect is roughly four times smaller than the smallest one E2 could have seen.
  • So E2's null does not falsify the model's hysteresis. It is a negative result about the test, not the model.
  • But one channel is not enough: σc cannot produce a calm-period baseline (0.0006 against a real 0.229), while a common shock in the prices matches the level and produces no crash rise (+0.008 against +0.127). A calibrated model needs both.

Results

Results
